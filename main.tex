\begin{abstract}
Serverless functions backed by SQLite face a persistent cold-start bottleneck. After idle periods, the OS page cache is evicted, making the first query orders of magnitude slower than subsequent queries. Existing prefetching techniques optimize this first-query latency, implicitly assuming it translates to faster end-to-end function response. This paper shows that assumption is systematically misleading, using a cost model that decomposes prefetch preprocessing into open and deliver terms under two deployment models, a warm-container that reuses an already-open handle and a standalone warmer that pays the cold open, and separates page selection from page delivery. All operations use standard OS interfaces and require no modification to SQLite. Three findings follow. First, the strategy that minimizes first-query latency, a full-working-set cache dump ($-79\%$ to $-91\%$), regresses end-to-end cold starts by about an order of magnitude on the broad-working-set workloads (Scattered-Zipf and Uniform-100K) due to a 0.8 to 7 ms deliver overhead, with the narrow small-working-set workload Tail-Mixed as the exception. Second, the robust selection principle is a \emph{mandatory} one: prefetching the small B+tree interior navigation skeleton yields roughly $25$ to $30\%$ warm-process end-to-end reduction across the evaluated pure-hit workloads with a footprint under 2 MB, whereas adding workload-specific hot leaves is an incremental optimization that helps only under a verified concentrated hotspot; a footprint-matched frequency-ranked dump is statistically indistinguishable from the type-aware selector, and leaf-frequency selection alone is essentially neutral once the estimator is corrected with a trace-order-independent tie-break. Under genuine Zipfian skew the leaf bonus is real, raising Scattered-Zipf from about $-25\%$ (interior only) to about $-36\%$. Third, a mixed tail-boundary workload whose key range is roughly half not-found high-key lookups produces a larger but narrowly scoped, bimodal result driven by a rightmost-leaf hotspot; a matched pure-hit control returns to the $\approx$$-27\%$ to $-30\%$ skeleton regime. Static frequency plans decay when the read hotspot moves, whereas structural coverage remains stable. The relative effectiveness ordering also carries over to a genuine FaaS runtime: on Apache OpenWhisk every strategy that is strongly effective on the workstation remains effective (41 of 41 first-query cells), and per-cell effectiveness ranks correlate at Spearman $\rho\approx0.76$ to $0.81$. To our knowledge, this is the first SQLite OS-page-cache cold-start evaluation to separate open, delivery, and first-query terms and align them with integrated and standalone deployment boundaries.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

\subsection{Background}\label{sec:background}

\subsubsection{Serverless SQLite as a Growing Deployment Target}\label{sec:serverless-sqlite}

Serverless computing has become a main model of execution for cloud applications, offering transparent scaling, fine-grained billing, and simplified operational management. An increasing number of serverless workloads are stateful and require access to persistent data stored in embedded databases co-located with function instances. Among embedded databases, SQLite stands out as the world's most widely deployed database engine. Gaffney et al. \cite{Gaffney2022} estimate that over one trillion SQLite databases are in active use and that the engine is embedded in nearly every smartphone, sensor, automobile, and television. The same study identifies edge computing as a key emerging use case for SQLite, where lightweight zero-configuration data management is essential. FaaS platforms such as AWS Lambda, Azure Functions, and Google Cloud Functions allow functions to mount persistent storage or use ephemeral local storage for SQLite databases, making SQLite a natural choice for serverless applications that require function-local state, configuration, and lightweight analytics. In such deployments, SQLite serves as the storage layer that supports serverless functions, and its performance directly impacts the response time of the entire function. Notably, production object-storage architectures interpose exactly this layer as a local cache tier: Cloudflare D1 caches object-stored databases on local disk to absorb query traffic~\cite{CloudflareD1}.

\subsubsection{The Warm Container, Cold Data Phenomenon}\label{sec:warm-container}

Wang et al. \cite{Wang2018} present the first large-scale measurement study of commercial serverless platforms, launching over 50,000 function instances across AWS Lambda, Azure Functions, and Google Cloud Functions. Their work reveals that serverless platforms rely on container or virtual machine reuse to manage resources efficiently, validating that the ``warm container'' state is a common deployment pattern in production. They also demonstrate that resource contention between co-located functions is common, providing a root cause for OS page cache eviction between invocations: when multiple functions compete for memory and I/O resources, the file-backed pages cached during one invocation may be reclaimed before the next invocation arrives. Shahrad et al. \cite{Shahrad2020} complement this analysis with a large-scale characterization of production FaaS workloads using Azure Functions trace data. Their study shows that invocation frequencies span eight orders of magnitude, with the vast majority of functions invoked infrequently, and that inter-invocation intervals are highly variable, making perfect prediction of future invocations impossible. To balance performance and cost, platforms employ keep-alive and prewarming strategies that keep function runtimes warm while allowing memory to be reclaimed during idle periods. The consequence is a ``warm container, cold data'' scenario: the function runtime and SQLite handle remain alive, but the OS page cache backing the database file has been evicted.

\subsection{Motivation}\label{sec:motivation}

The histogram-based optimization proposed by Shahrad et al. \cite{Shahrad2020} improves the accuracy of prewarming, meaning that more invocations hit warm containers. However, as this optimization succeeds, the bottleneck shifts from container startup to the storage layer: the function runtime is warm, but the OS page cache remains cold. This creates a new performance frontier where container-level optimizations have diminishing returns, and the remaining cold-start penalty resides entirely in the data path.

Du et al. \cite{Du2020} further demonstrate this trend with Catalyzer, achieving sub-millisecond function startup through checkpoint/restore and a new OS primitive called sfork. While their work validates state reuse as a powerful technique for reducing container-level cold starts, it does not address the OS page cache. The file-backed pages cached during one invocation are not preserved from checkpoint, so even with sub-millisecond container startup, the first database query after a cold cache still incurs disk I/O. The better container-level cold-start optimization becomes, the more the remaining bottleneck resides in the data path.

This is the problem we address. On our reference SQLite database, the first query under a cold OS page cache takes 523 to 1087 \textmu s, compared to 1 to 3 \textmu s when the cache is warm, a 200$\times$ to 1000$\times$ slowdown. This places the storage-layer cold-start penalty in the same latency regime as container startup itself (sub-millisecond), meaning that even with optimal container-level warming, the data path remains a first-order bottleneck.

The stakes rise further in object-storage-backed deployments. The root-to-interior-to-leaf fault chain is serial and dependent: each fault must complete before the next page address is known. On local NVMe each link costs microseconds. When SQLite is served from object storage via HTTP range requests~\cite{SqliteWebVfs, Turbolite}, each link becomes a multi-millisecond GET, amplifying the same chain by three to four orders of magnitude. Our measurements quantify this chain at the local-storage tier; the structure of the penalty, and the cost-accounting methodology for attacking it, carry over.

\subsection{Challenges and Problem Statement}\label{sec:challenges}

Prefetching the critical B+tree pages before the first query executes seems a natural solution to the cold-data problem. Yet existing approaches to SQLite prefetching face three fundamental challenges that have not been simultaneously addressed.

\textbf{Challenge 1: Targeting.} The OS page cache operates at the granularity of file blocks, with no understanding of SQLite's internal B+tree structure. OS-level readahead detects only sequential access patterns and misses the non-sequential root-to-leaf traversal path that characterizes database queries. Application-level techniques that blindly cache entire working sets waste I/O on pages irrelevant to the critical first query. Without visibility into SQLite's page types, prefetching either misses the target entirely or overshoots with wasteful I/O.

\textbf{Challenge 2: Cost Accounting.} Even when the right pages are identified, prefetching is not free. Prior work optimizes first-query latency under the implicit assumption that improvement there translates directly to faster end-to-end performance. In the serverless critical path, however, a user request blocks on the function's response; any time spent on prefetching directly extends the response latency. This preprocessing overhead consists of OS-syscall-level operations, including cold file open and per-page deliver, whose cost scales with the size of the prefetch set. When this overhead exceeds the first-query latency reduction, prefetching makes cold starts slower, not faster.

\textbf{Challenge 3: Deployability.} Serverless environments impose strict execution constraints. Functions run in restricted contexts where modifying the runtime, OS kernel, or database engine is often infeasible. Existing solutions that require deep forks of SQLite, custom FTL modifications, or kernel-level extensions are not deployable in production FaaS settings. A practical solution must operate entirely at the application layer, using only standard OS interfaces, without privileged access.

These three challenges are interdependent. Solving any two without the third yields a solution that is either ineffective (poor targeting), misleading (ignored overhead), or impractical (infeasible to deploy). This paper addresses all three simultaneously.

\subsection{Our Contribution}\label{sec:contribution}

This paper presents a frugal, non-intrusive SQLite prefetch framework for serverless cold starts that systematically addresses the triple challenge outlined above. Our contributions are:

\textbf{Contribution 1: Skeleton-first targeted prefetching with a conditional leaf bonus.} We show that the mandatory B+tree interior navigation skeleton is the robust default prefetch target: interior-only prefetching yields roughly $25$ to $30\%$ warm-process end-to-end reduction across the evaluated pure-hit workloads with a footprint under 2 MB. Adding workload-specific hot leaves is an \emph{incremental} optimization that pays off only under a verified concentrated hotspot. A corrected ablation (whose estimator we fix with a trace-order-independent tie-break, Section~\ref{sec:eval-attribution}) shows that random leaves can slow end-to-end latency, that leaf-frequency selection alone is essentially a tie, and that a footprint-matched frequency-ranked dump is statistically indistinguishable from the type-aware selector; leaf prefetch does not substitute for a cold interior path. We also evaluate a layout rewriter that clusters interior pages at the file head as a design alternative and find it a negative result under our workloads. [\textit{Addresses Challenge 1: Targeting.}]

\textbf{Contribution 2: OS-syscall-level cost accounting.} We decompose prefetch preprocessing into its constituent OS-syscall-level terms: a cold file open (approximately 230 \textmu s, per-layout constant) and per-page deliver operations, whose cost scales with the size of the prefetch set. We evaluate end-to-end latency under two deployment models directly aligned with serverless execution patterns: a standalone warmer that pays the cold open cost, and a warm-container model that reuses an already-open SQLite handle. This methodology exposes, to our knowledge for the first time, the systematic first-query versus end-to-end trade-off in SQLite cold-start prefetching. [\textit{Addresses Challenge 2: Cost Accounting.}]

\textbf{Contribution 3: Non-intrusive, deployment-compatible toolchain.} Our framework operates entirely at the application layer using only standard POSIX interfaces (\texttt{mmap}, \texttt{mincore}, \texttt{madvise}). It requires no modification to SQLite, the OS kernel, the FTL, or the serverless runtime, and does not require privileged access or root permissions. These properties make the framework compatible with the execution restrictions of production FaaS environments without requiring platform modification or privileged access. [\textit{Addresses Challenge 3: Deployability.}]

\textbf{Rigorous empirical validation.} We validate our framework across six robustness axes: write churn that perturbs page layout (50k mutations across 11 checkpoints), sub-working-set RAM pressure that triggers page reclaim, multi-process cache sharing with cadence-based re-warm, workload instantiation with 10 independent random seeds and bootstrap 95\% confidence intervals, database scaling by a factor of 10 (102 MB to approximately 1 GiB), and static-plan staleness under a moving read hotspot. We further establish cross-platform effectiveness portability to a genuine FaaS runtime: on Apache OpenWhisk every strongly-effective workstation strategy remains effective (41 of 41 first-query cells) across all 65 covered cells, with per-cell effectiveness ranks correlating at Spearman $\rho\approx0.76$ to $0.81$. This multi-dimensional validation ensures that our headline results are not artifacts of a single workload sample, deployment platform, or a particular hardware configuration.

Our results reveal a counter-intuitive but robust finding. The strategy that minimizes first-query latency the most, a cache-dump approach that reloads the entire resident working set, regresses end-to-end performance by roughly an order of magnitude on the broad-working-set workloads Scattered-Zipf and Uniform-100K, where its deliver overhead of 0.8 to 7 ms dominates the response time; the narrow small-working-set workload Tail-Mixed is the exception. In contrast, a frugal prefetch of the interior navigation skeleton, with an aggregate footprint under 2 MB, delivers consistent, statistically robust warm-process end-to-end improvements across the evaluated pure-hit workloads, and a small hotspot-conditional leaf budget adds incremental value only where a workload is genuinely skewed. This work is, to the best of our knowledge, the first to separate open, delivery, and first-query terms and align them with integrated and standalone deployment boundaries in SQLite-backed serverless cold starts, with an application-layer design that operates within the execution constraints of restricted FaaS environments without privileged platform changes.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section~\ref{sec:related} surveys related work and positions our contributions. Section~\ref{sec:fundamentals} establishes the SQLite B+tree storage structure and our warm-container, cold-data measurement model. Section~\ref{sec:methodology} presents our end-to-end cost model, selection--delivery decomposition, measurement protocol, and statistical methodology. Section~\ref{sec:strategy} describes the two-lever prefetch strategies (a mandatory interior skeleton and a conditional hot-leaf bonus) and the physical layout alternatives we evaluate. Section~\ref{sec:setup} details our experimental platform, reference database, and workloads. Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} reports our experimental results, centered on the first-query versus end-to-end trade-off. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses the scope and limitations of our findings, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes with future directions.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

\subsection{OS-Level Prefetching and Disk Scheduling}\label{sec:os-prefetch}

The foundation of OS-level prefetching traces back to Smith \cite{Smith1978}, who first quantified sequentiality in database access patterns and proposed One Block Lookahead (OBL). His work established that prefetching can reduce I/O latency by exploiting sequential access patterns, and introduced a formal cost model to determine optimal prefetch strategies. However, this approach relies on history-based prediction: it observes past access patterns to infer future ones, incurring the risk of misprediction that pollutes the cache with unused data. In contrast, our cold-start scenario has no meaningful history for the first query path, and our approach derives the necessary page set from SQLite's B+tree structure directly, without forecasting future accesses. We use \texttt{mincore} to observe what is already resident and \texttt{madvise} to prefetch what is structurally required, combining observation with deduction rather than prediction.

Iyer and Druschel \cite{Iyer2001} present a complementary challenge at the OS scheduling layer. They identify ``deceptive idleness'' in synchronous I/O workloads: work-conserving disk schedulers switch away from a process too quickly after its request completes because the process's next request is delayed by a short computation phase. Their anticipatory scheduling framework addresses this by deliberately waiting a short interval after each request, anticipating that the same process may issue another nearby request. While effective on HDDs where seek time dominates, on modern NVMe storage where random access costs microseconds rather than milliseconds, the seek-avoidance premise no longer holds. Their work highlights a fundamental limitation: the OS, lacking application-level visibility into when the next request will arrive, can only trade time for locality. Our approach takes the complementary direction: rather than having the OS guess, we provide it with explicit structural information (B+tree page types) so that prefetching decisions are driven by deterministic knowledge rather than speculation.

Together, these foundational works establish a recurring theme in OS-level I/O optimization: the OS operates with limited visibility into application-level semantics, forcing it to rely on history-based prediction or time-based speculation. Our work continues this line of inquiry but pivots from prediction to deduction, using SQLite's B+tree structure as a source of deterministic knowledge that eliminates both the need for history and the risk of misprediction.

\subsection{Buffer-Pool Warming and Learned Prefetching}\label{sec:buffer-warming}

The foundation of database buffer management was established by Effelsberg and Härder \cite{Effelsberg1984}, who formalized the three core tasks of any buffer manager: buffer search, frame allocation, and page replacement, and systematized the FIX-UNFIX interface that subsequent buffer-pool warming and prefetching approaches have adopted as a foundational abstraction.

Chen et al. \cite{Chen2021} propose an end-to-end deep learning framework for database page prefetching, framing page access prediction as a classification problem. They evaluate DNN, CNN, RNN, LSTM, and a Multi-Model ensemble with 8 to 20 million parameters on real-world database benchmarks, showing that ML-based models achieve higher prediction accuracy (76 to 87\% precision/recall) than heuristic-based baselines (LookAhead at 20\%). However, their evaluation focuses on prediction accuracy rather than end-to-end query latency impact. Critically, they acknowledge that ``wrong prefetching, though asynchronous, will hurt the performance of the system due to the extra I/O cost'' and design a Decision Module to mitigate this, but their experimental evaluation does not quantify this cost, neither the inference overhead of the neural network nor the wasted I/O from mis-prefetched pages. Furthermore, their training traces are explicitly collected with warm-start, which is appropriate for steady-state pattern learning but renders their findings inapplicable to cold-start scenarios where no historical trace exists for training.

Yi et al. \cite{Yi2026} propose Pre-Buffer, a workload-aware buffer prefetching framework that addresses what they term the ``buffer cold-start problem'': the prolonged drop in buffer hit rate when data access hotspots shift dramatically. Observing that many real-world workloads exhibit periodic patterns, Pre-Buffer records hot pages from each workload cycle and, upon detecting a workload change, uses similarity metrics (e.g., Jaccard similarity) to predict the hot page set of the new workload. An independent background prefetching thread then loads these predicted hot pages into the buffer in advance. This work is the closest to ours in terms of problem awareness: both address cold-start scenarios in database systems, and both recognize that prefetching has overhead that must be considered. In fact, Yi et al. explicitly criticize Chen et al. \cite{Chen2021} for ignoring prefetch overhead, stating that ``it is also necessary to consider the direct and indirect impact of the prefetch module on system performance.'' However, their evaluation remains at the level of hit-rate recovery time and total execution time, without separating prefetch overhead from query latency. Critically, Pre-Buffer uses a background thread so its overhead does not directly extend user-facing latency. In our serverless cold-start scenario, prefetch runs on the critical path before the first query; any preprocessing overhead directly contributes to end-to-end response time. This distinction makes cost accounting not a secondary concern but a first-order requirement, and motivates our OS-syscall-level decomposition of prefetch preprocessing into open and deliver terms.

Yang et al. \cite{Yang2020} present Leaper, a learned prefetcher for LSM-tree storage engines, which predicts hot records to mitigate cache invalidation caused by compaction operations. Its effectiveness depends on access skew, aligning with our observation in Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} that hotspot-based selection yields no benefit under uniform workloads where no natural hot set exists.

Funke et al. \cite{Funke2022} identify a mirror trade-off in query compilation: just-in-time compilation offers high execution speed but introduces compilation latencies that can dominate overall response time for short-running or complex queries. Their lightweight domain-specific IR (Flounder IR) reduces compilation times by up to two orders of magnitude over LLVM, explicitly quantifying the break-even point where compilation cost outweighs execution benefit. This mirrors our core observation in the I/O domain: optimizing the execution phase (first-query latency) does not guarantee end-to-end acceleration if the preparation phase carries non-trivial overhead. Their solution, a lightweight IR replacing heavyweight LLVM, exhibits structural symmetry with our approach: frugal \texttt{madvise} hints and a small hotset replacing heavy cache dumps. Both works share the high-level insight that on the critical path, preprocessing cost must be explicitly quantified.

\subsection{Application-Directed Prefetching}\label{sec:app-directed}

VanDeBogart et al. \cite{VanDeBogart2009} propose libprefetch, a library that allows applications to explicitly communicate their future read access patterns to the OS via a callback-based interface. By accumulating a large reorder buffer and issuing disk requests in C-LOOK order (scanning forward and seeking back only when no requests remain ahead), libprefetch minimizes seek distance and achieves substantial speedups on HDD-bound workloads. Their evaluation demonstrates that application-directed prefetching can accelerate SQLite table scans by up to 20$\times$ and GIMP image processing by up to 4.9$\times$, confirming that application-level knowledge of access patterns can dramatically improve I/O performance compared to OS-level readahead, which lacks such semantic information. Their approach explicitly demonstrates the value of bridging the semantic gap between application and storage layers: when the application knows its future accesses, the OS can make better I/O scheduling decisions.

However, libprefetch differs from our work in three critical aspects that reflect changes in both hardware and deployment constraints over the past decade. First, its design is optimized for HDD seek-time reduction. On HDDs, seek time dominates I/O latency (5–10 ms per seek), making large reorder buffers and request reordering highly effective. On modern NVMe storage, seek time is negligible; the bottleneck shifts to page-cache miss latency and the cost of OS-level operations, which operate at a vastly different scale. This difference in physical constraints changes the optimal prefetching strategy entirely: libprefetch aggressively prefetches as much data as memory allows (reorder buffers of hundreds of megabytes), whereas our approach must be frugal because each syscall and each prefetched page directly contributes to end-to-end latency on the critical path.

Second, libprefetch requires modifying application source code to inject access-pattern callbacks. In their SQLite integration, this amounts to approximately 500 lines of changes to the SQLite source to examine the secondary index and generate the access list of table data pages. This violates the non-intrusive deployment constraint that serverless environments demand, where functions run in restricted contexts and modifying the underlying engine is often infeasible. Our framework operates entirely at the application layer without any modification to SQLite, using only standard OS interfaces (\texttt{mmap}, \texttt{mincore}, \texttt{madvise}) that are universally available.

Third, while libprefetch's reported end-to-end runtimes do include prefetch time, its evaluation regime does not require decomposing that cost: on HDDs, seek time dominates at the millisecond scale, making microsecond-level syscall overhead negligible. In our serverless cold-start critical path, however, the first query completes in hundreds of microseconds, and overhead of comparable magnitude can no longer be ignored. This cost accounting distinction is not merely quantitative but qualitative: it changes the optimization objective from maximizing I/O throughput to minimizing end-to-end critical-path latency.

In summary, libprefetch establishes that application-directed prefetching is a powerful technique when access patterns are known in advance. Our work inherits this high-level insight but applies it to a fundamentally different regime: NVMe-based serverless cold starts, where the prefetch itself is on the critical path, deployment constraints prohibit source modifications, and end-to-end latency is the primary metric.

\subsection{SQLite and mmap: Virtual-Memory Assisted Caching}\label{sec:mmap}

The use of memory-mapped I/O (\texttt{mmap}) in database systems has been the subject of sustained debate over the past decade. Crotty et al. \cite{Crotty2022} deliver a forceful critique of using \texttt{mmap} as a replacement for a traditional DBMS buffer pool. They identify four fundamental problems: transaction atomicity and crash recovery become impossible to guarantee because \texttt{mmap}'s writeback timing is outside DBMS control; I/O behavior becomes unpredictable; error handling is difficult because memory-mapped I/O errors manifest as signals; and performance scalability suffers, particularly on fast NVMe devices where the \texttt{mmap} path cannot match user-space I/O management. Their experimental analysis demonstrates clear performance limitations of \texttt{mmap} relative to user-space I/O control. However, they explicitly note a narrow set of conditions under which \texttt{mmap} is defensible: read-only workloads where the working set fits in memory. Our cold-start prefetch scenario falls squarely within this exception: the first query is read-only, and our reference database (102 MB) is well within main memory on our experimental platform. More broadly, as we demonstrate in Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}, our frugal hotset (under 2 MB) fits comfortably within even the most constrained serverless memory limits. Our cgroup RAM pressure experiments further stress this condition, showing that our targeted hotset maintains 100\% delivery even under sub-working-set memory caps, while cache-dump approaches collapse.

Critically, our use of \texttt{mmap} is fundamentally different from the DBMS-as-substrate scenario that Crotty et al. critique. We do not use \texttt{mmap} as a buffer pool replacement; SQLite's own pager and B+tree manage transaction semantics, caching, and recovery. Instead, we use \texttt{mmap} exclusively as a lightweight prefetch hint channel: \texttt{mmap} establishes the read-only mapping, \texttt{mincore} queries page residency in the OS page cache, and \texttt{madvise(MADV\_WILLNEED)} issues prefetch hints to the OS. This is an application-layer tool that does not interfere with SQLite's storage management. The \texttt{mmap} path also offers a practical advantage in serverless environments: by avoiding user-space data copying, it reduces memory footprint at a time when every megabyte counts.

Leis et al. \cite{Leis2023} extend this line of work in a complementary direction. They propose vmcache, a buffer manager design that uses hardware-supported virtual memory to translate page identifiers to virtual addresses while retaining DBMS control over eviction via \texttt{madvise(MADV\_DONTNEED)}. However, they find that vanilla Linux's page-table manipulation primitives do not scale to fast NVMe devices and multi-core CPUs, with TLB shootdowns consuming up to 79\% of CPU time at 1.5M ops/s. They therefore propose exmap, a Linux kernel module that implements scalable page table manipulation. Together, vmcache and exmap provide efficient buffer management for high-throughput, steady-state workloads.

This body of work forms a complete design space with our approach. Crotty et al. identify the problem with \texttt{mmap} as a DBMS substrate. Leis et al. solve it for high-frequency, high-throughput scenarios by modifying the kernel. Our work occupies the orthogonal corner of the design space: we operate in the low-frequency (approximately 92 \texttt{madvise} calls per cold-start), no-root, application-layer regime where standard OS interfaces are entirely sufficient. The three works together cover the full spectrum: from critique (Crotty+22), to kernel-level solution for steady-state (Leis+23), to our application-level solution for cold-start (our work).

\subsection{SQLite Write-Path Optimizations}\label{sec:write-path}

A separate line of work has focused on optimizing SQLite's write throughput in mobile and embedded environments. Oh et al. \cite{Oh2015} address write amplification in mobile applications with autocommit write workloads, where successive page writes and journaling cause throughput degradation. Their solution requires deep modifications to SQLite's B+tree module, pager, buffer management policy, and journaling, complemented by custom phase-change memory hardware. Kang et al. \cite{Kang2013} take a different approach, placing transaction semantics at the FTL layer (X-FTL) to reduce SQLite's journaling overhead. Kim et al. \cite{Kim2012} and Jeong et al. \cite{Jeong2013} provide foundational analyses of mobile storage performance, establishing the write path as the dominant bottleneck in SQLite-on-flash deployments.

These works share a common focus on improving write throughput and write amplification in resource-constrained environments. They are therefore orthogonal to our work, which targets read cold-start latency in serverless deployments. Critically, these write-path optimizations require invasive modifications to SQLite engine internals or storage firmware, which are infeasible in serverless environments where functions run in restricted contexts with no access to underlying engine code. Our framework operates entirely at the application layer and requires no modification to SQLite, the OS kernel, or the storage stack.

This write-centric focus further underscores a gap in the SQLite literature. Gaffney et al. \cite{Gaffney2022}, the most comprehensive academic evaluation of SQLite to date, explicitly describe their measurement protocol: ``Our only departure from the specification is that before running the SSB queries, we scan each table with a \texttt{SELECT *} query, ensuring that the buffer pool is populated.'' This prewarming step is entirely appropriate for their analytical throughput objectives, but it also illustrates a broader pattern: cold-start latency is treated as noise to be eliminated rather than a metric to be measured. The standard evaluation methodology in the SQLite community systematically renders cold-start invisible. The same principle that makes their Lookahead Information Passing optimization effective, that structural knowledge outperforms blind mechanisms, motivates our approach: we use storage structure knowledge, specifically B+tree page-type awareness, to address the cold-start phase, complementing their steady-state optimization with a cold-start counterpart on the same engine. The combination of write-path optimization and warm-start evaluation protocols leaves read cold-start latency as an unexplored dimension of SQLite performance, precisely the gap we address.

\subsection{Serverless Cold-Start}\label{sec:serverless-coldstart}

As established in Section~\ref{sec:warm-container}, the serverless cold-start problem has been extensively characterized at the platform level. Wang et al. \cite{Wang2018} revealed that container reuse is an industry-wide practice across major FaaS providers, and Shahrad et al. \cite{Shahrad2020} demonstrated that invocation intervals are highly variable, making perfect prediction impossible. These studies establish the empirical foundation for our ``warm container, cold data'' scenario.

A separate line of work has addressed serverless cold starts at the container level. Du et al. \cite{Du2020} propose Catalyzer, achieving sub-millisecond function startup through checkpoint/restore and a new OS primitive called sfork (sandbox fork). Their work demonstrates that state reuse is a powerful technique for reducing startup latency, and Catalyzer has been adopted by Ant Financial in production, providing strong evidence that container-level state reuse is a practically validated direction. However, Catalyzer addresses the compute layer; it does not preserve the OS page cache across invocations. The file-backed pages cached during one invocation are not restored from checkpoint, so even with sub-millisecond container startup, the first database query after a cold cache still incurs disk I/O. Our work is complementary: we address the storage-layer cold start that persists after container-level optimizations have done their work, with the additional advantage of operating entirely at the application layer without kernel modifications.

At the storage layer, a growing ecosystem serves SQLite at scale in serverless environments, from per-tenant edge databases~\cite{CloudflareD1} to functions querying object-stored databases through a custom VFS that translates each page I/O into an HTTP range GET~\cite{SqliteWebVfs, Litestream, SqlJsHttpvfs, Turbolite}; in this ecosystem, cold start is the headline metric~\cite{Turbolite}. Most relevant to our work is the \texttt{.dbi} helper of \texttt{sqlite\_web\_vfs}~\cite{SqliteWebVfs}: it collects navigation-critical pages (B-tree interiors and metadata tables) into a compact side file that the VFS prefetches, avoiding scattered reads from the main database. This validates interior-skeleton prefetching in production, and differs from our work in three ways. First, \texttt{.dbi} is a separate sidecar file for a read-only HTTP VFS, whereas our framework operates on the local OS page cache without introducing additional files and supports read-write workloads. Second, no published cost accounting of the prefetch itself exists for the \texttt{.dbi} approach, which is precisely the gap our framework addresses. Third, \texttt{.dbi} selection stops at page types, matching our robust interior-skeleton lever; we additionally evaluate an access-frequency leaf lever, which Section~\ref{sec:eval-attribution} shows adds value only under a verified concentrated hotspot. The mechanisms occupy different storage tiers (OS page cache versus VFS-over-HTTP), but the cost-accounting methodology and the skeleton-first selection principle transfer.

\section{SQLite Cold-Start Fundamentals}\label{sec:fundamentals}

\subsection{B+tree Storage and Page Types}\label{sec:btree}

SQLite stores an entire database in a single file, organized internally as fixed-size pages of 4 KB. Each logical table or index corresponds to one B+tree. Pages are classified into two roles: \textit{interior pages} store keys and child pointers, while \textit{leaf pages} contain the actual row data or index entries. The architecture is detailed in the authoritative SQLite evaluation by Gaffney et al.~\cite{Gaffney2022}. In our reference database of 600,000 rows (102 MB), there are 92 interior pages, which is 51 table interior and 41 index interior, totaling 368 KB and 26,239 leaf pages. A subtle but important detail is that SQLite page 1 holds the database header and the root of the \texttt{sqlite\_master} schema B+tree; the root page of each user table is stored in the \texttt{rootpage} field of \texttt{sqlite\_master} and is typically a low page number, not necessarily page 1.

The structural arithmetic that follows from standard B+tree fanout is simple but consequential: interior pages constitute only 0.35\% of the database (368 KB of 102 MB), yet every query must traverse from root to leaf, and thus every query requires at least the interior pages along its path. For a single point query, only the pages on one root-to-leaf path approximately the tree height, typically 3 to 4 pages must reside in memory. Over a full workload, the accumulated set of interior pages that are visited converges to the complete set of 92. This distinction between a single query's path and the full workload's interior set is critical: it explains why a frugal prefetch can target only a few pages for a specific query, while a workload-aware strategy might need the full interior skeleton. This structural fact is not a novel discovery, it is a direct consequence of B+tree fanout arithmetic but it provides the theoretical foundation for our targeted prefetch approach: the pages that dominate cold-start cost are both few in number and universally required.

\subsection{Cold-Start Mechanics}\label{sec:coldstart-mechanics}

When the OS page cache is empty, SQLite's first query must traverse the B+tree from root to leaf by fetching every missing page from disk. Each interior page that is absent from the cache triggers a random disk read; on commodity NVMe storage, each such fault typically incurs 5 to 100 \textmu s of latency. The cumulative effect of these sequential page faults, each blocking the query's progress, produces the cold-start slowdown quantified in Section~\ref{sec:motivation} (523 to 1087 \textmu s cold vs. 1 to 3 \textmu s warm, a 200$\times$ to 1000$\times$ penalty).

The root cause is structural: without prefetching, the query pays the full cost of fetching every page on the critical path, one fault at a time. The opportunity is equally structural: because the interior page set is small and known in advance, a prefetch that brings these pages into the OS page cache before the first query executes could eliminate these faults entirely. Whether such prefetching actually improves end-to-end performance, however, depends on two factors that are the subjects of the following sections: which pages to target (Section~\ref{sec:strategy}) and what preprocessing cost the prefetch itself incurs (Section~\ref{sec:methodology}).

\subsection{The Warm-Container, Cold-Data Measurement Model}\label{sec:measurement-model}

Section~\ref{sec:warm-container} established that the ``warm container, cold data'' state arises systematically in production serverless deployments: platforms keep function runtimes warm via container reuse and keep-alive policies, while the OS page cache backing the database file is evicted during idle periods. Our measurement model instantiates exactly this state. We keep the process-layer long-lived structures—the SQLite handle, prepared statements, and the \texttt{mmap} mapping—warm across measurements, while clearing every software cache below the process layer before each measurement. This models the serverless wake-up scenario where the function runtime is alive but the database pages have been reclaimed.

This design reflects three modeling choices that are deliberate rather than compromises. First, \textit{fidelity to deployment}: serverless platforms that reuse containers preserve the runtime and handle state; the only layer that must be re-established is the OS page cache. Keeping the process warm while clearing the page cache directly mirrors this architecture. Second, \textit{variable isolation}: SQLite parser and optimizer startup time is a constant overhead independent of prefetch strategy; including it would add noise without illuminating the prefetch mechanism. Third, \textit{reproducibility}: starting a fresh process for each measurement introduces 50 to 200 \textmu s of initialization noise, requiring more repetitions to suppress; our protocol reduces this variance, yielding cleaner comparisons across strategies.

\section{Cold-Start Cost Model and Measurement Methodology}\label{sec:methodology}

\subsection{The End-to-End Cost Model}\label{sec:cost-model}

The first-query latency $t_{\text{fq}}$ captures only the SQL query's wall-clock time. It does not include the preprocessing overhead of issuing prefetch hints. From the user's perspective, however, the cold-start latency that matters is the end-to-end time from function wake-up to query completion. Any time spent on prefetching directly extends the response latency. In serverless deployments, this distinction is particularly acute: a user request blocks on the function's response, so every microsecond of preprocessing contributes directly to perceived latency.

We decompose prefetch preprocessing into two OS-syscall-level terms:

\begin{itemize}
    \item $t_{\text{open}}$: the cost of a cold file open on the SQLite database, approximately 230~\textmu s, constant for a given layout and independent of the prefetch strategy.
    \item $t_{\text{deliver}}$: the per-page cost of issuing prefetch operations, whether \texttt{madvise} hints or synchronous \texttt{pread} calls. This term scales linearly with the size of the hotset.
\end{itemize}

The warmer reports both terms separately (as \texttt{open\_us} and \texttt{deliver\_us} in the harness output), with the total preprocessing time $t_{\text{prep}} = t_{\text{open}} + t_{\text{deliver}}$. The end-to-end cold-start latency is then the sum of preprocessing and the first-query latency itself. We define two deployment models that directly correspond to serverless execution patterns:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:e2e_std}
    \text{e2e}_{\text{standalone}} = t_{\text{open}} + t_{\text{deliver}} + t_{\text{fq}}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}\label{eq:e2e_warm}
    \text{e2e}_{\text{warm}} = t_{\text{deliver}} + t_{\text{fq}}
\end{equation}

where $t_{\text{fq}}$ is the first-query latency measured by the harness.

The two models differ only by the presence of $t_{\text{open}}$:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Standalone warmer (Eq.~\ref{eq:e2e_std})} corresponds to an external warmer process—for example, a sidecar that performs prefetching and must cold-open the database file independently. This is the pessimistic upper bound, modeling an external prefetching agent rather than an integrated deployment.
    \item \textbf{Warm-process / integrated (Eq.~\ref{eq:e2e_warm})} corresponds to the ``warm container, cold data'' scenario instantiated in Section~\ref{sec:measurement-model}: the application is already running, the SQLite handle is already open, and prefetch reuses the existing handle without paying a second cold open. This is the model we advocate for serverless keep-alive and edge worker deployments.
\end{itemize}

Three measurements support this cost model. First, $t_{\text{fq}}$ is measured directly by the harness. Second, $t_{\text{open}}$ and $t_{\text{deliver}}$ are self-reported by the warmer; the same engine is used for both \texttt{pread} and async modes, differing only in delivery mechanism. Third, the baseline first-query latency uses the handle opened before cache clearing (Section~\ref{sec:measurement-model}), so the baseline contains no \texttt{open} cost under either deployment model. The baseline is thus identical for both models: $\text{baseline} = t_{\text{fq}}$ with no preprocessing.

The hotset itself is generated offline by a separate pipeline: a cold clear, a workload warmup, and a \texttt{mincore} snapshot. This generation is a one-time or periodic cost that is amortized across many cold starts. As we show in the churn experiments of Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}, on our stationary read workloads the hotset does not decay under 50k write mutations, so recomputation is infrequent there; a read hotspot that itself moves over time is a separate axis (Section~\ref{sec:eval-robustness}), under which a static frequency plan does need refreshing. We therefore do not include this offline selection cost in the per-event critical-path end-to-end latency, which includes only delivery and the first query.

The trade-off quantified by this cost model is the foundation of our core observation in Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}: when $t_{\text{deliver}}$ is small relative to $t_{\text{fq}}$, prefetching improves end-to-end performance; when $t_{\text{deliver}}$ dominates, even the best first-query reduction can make cold starts slower.

\subsection{Selection--Delivery Decomposition}\label{sec:selection-delivery}

The \texttt{madvise(MADV\_WILLNEED)} hint is asynchronous and does not guarantee that pages are resident by the time the first query executes. The kernel records the hint and initiates background readahead, then returns immediately. Whether a particular page is actually loaded before the first query depends on scheduling, I/O completion timing, and the kernel's readahead window. Consequently, the observed first-query latency under async hints conflates two orthogonal factors: the accuracy of the prefetch set selected by the targeting strategy (\textit{selection}) and the degree to which that set is actually resident when the query starts (\textit{delivery}). Our methodology isolates these factors by running each hotset under two delivery modes, summarized in Table~\ref{tab:delivery-modes}.

\centering
\begin{tabular}{>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.2\linewidth}
                >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.30\linewidth}
                >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.35\linewidth}}
\toprule
\textbf{Mode} & \textbf{Behavior} & \textbf{Measures} \\
\midrule
\texttt{pread} (oracle) & Synchronous blocking read; returns only when all pages in the hotset are resident & $t_{\text{fq,pread}}$: the lower bound on first-query latency achievable if the selected hotset is perfectly delivered \\

The oracle mode is not a deployable strategy; its synchronous nature imposes millisecond-scale preprocessing that would defeat the purpose of low-latency cold starts. It serves as a lower bound on first-query latency: $t_{\text{fq,pread}}$ represents the best possible first-query latency achievable if the selected hotset is fully resident before the query starts. The async mode captures the practical case. The gap between them is the delivery loss:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:delivery_loss}
    \text{delivery\_loss} = t_{\text{fq,async}} - t_{\text{fq,pread}}
\end{equation}

Real deployments fall between these extremes. Bare async hints provide the lower bound on preprocessing effort, no synchronous I/O, but may leave pages unloaded. At the other end, synchronous delivery guarantees residency at the cost of blocking the critical path. Intermediate mechanisms, such as issuing hints earlier in the function lifecycle, using \texttt{readahead(2)} with explicit control, or employing \texttt{io\_uring} for non-blocking but guaranteed I/O, can shift the delivery rate toward the oracle bound. Our evaluation focuses on the pure async case as the most widely deployable baseline; the oracle bound defines the performance ceiling that stronger delivery mechanisms could approach.

The kernel's readahead window caps the amount of data loaded by a single \texttt{madvise(WILLNEED)} range. With the default \texttt{read\_ahead\_kb} = 128 on our platform, a single range hint can theoretically load at most approximately $2 \times (128 / 4) = 64$ pages. In practice, on the scattered layout, we observe only 32 of the 92 requested pages delivered for a single range hint; range-based hints on scattered layouts thus deliver only a fraction of the requested set. Our preferred strategies, the per-page targeted strategies that we define in Section~\ref{sec:strategy}, issue per-page hints rather than range hints, so each page is requested individually; they therefore avoid this window limitation entirely. All measurements are conducted with \texttt{read\_ahead\_kb} fixed at the system default of 128 KB, which maximizes external validity; we record this parameter with every run and do not sweep other values in this study.

One potential concern is that our harness measures the async first-query latency immediately after the warmer returns, giving the kernel no extra time to complete background I/O. This tight timing could, in principle, overestimate the delivery loss: if a real application had additional work to perform between prefetching and the first query, readahead might finish in time. We directly tested this possibility with an intermediate-delivery sweep, inserting sleeps of 5, 20, and 50 ms between the hint dispatch and the first query on Scattered-Zipf and Tail-Mixed with representative strategies (\texttt{Skel-5}, \texttt{Skel}, and \texttt{Skel+10}, defined in Section~\ref{sec:strategy}). The results confirm two findings. First, the hotset is already 100\% resident at sleep=0: the per-page hints deliver the entire set before the harness moves to the query, so there is no outstanding I/O to wait for. Second, the residual gap $t_{\text{fq,async}} - t_{\text{fq,pread}}$ on Scattered-Zipf remains stable across all sleep durations, and the major fault counts are identical between the two modes. This gap is therefore a real and persistent cost difference, not a measurement artifact caused by insufficient post-hint delay. The exact mechanism underlying this gap remains a labeled conjecture; it may reflect the fact that synchronous \texttt{pread} warms the kernel's readahead state more effectively than one-shot async hints, but confirming this hypothesis requires root access to sweep \texttt{read\_ahead\_kb}, which our experimental environment lacks. We therefore conclude only that the gap is real and measurable, and leave its root cause to future investigation.

The CPU frequency is pinned via EPP performance mode; we verified boost behavior directly to ensure that the frequency ramps to maximum before each timed measurement. The hotset for each strategy is frozen via a checksum and verified by the harness before use, ensuring that selection results are deterministic and do not drift across runs.

\subsection{Statistical Methodology}\label{sec:stats}

Each (workload, layout, strategy, delivery mode) cell is repeated 10 times (n=10 reps) in rep-major order, meaning all 10 repetitions of a cell are run consecutively before moving to the next cell, which reduces cross-cell environmental drift. We report the median for each cell, as it is more robust than the mean for cold-start latency distributions, which exhibit long tails due to random I/O variation. Because the cost-model terms are additive within a single repetition, we compute each end-to-end quantity (Equations~\ref{eq:e2e_std} and~\ref{eq:e2e_warm}) \emph{per repetition}, pairing each repetition's $t_{\text{deliver}}$ and $t_{\text{fq}}$ from the same cold event, and report the median over the ten per-repetition end-to-end values. We do \emph{not} sum the per-term medians. Where decomposition tables and stacked-bar figures show the individual terms ($t_{\text{open}}$, $t_{\text{deliver}}$, $t_{\text{fq}}$), those per-term medians are given for attribution only and may differ slightly from the reported end-to-end median, because the median is not additive. We also report the 95th percentile and standard deviation where applicable. All improvement percentages are computed via paired comparison against the baseline from the same batch; this eliminates cross-batch absolute drift from the effect size calculation.

The effect sizes we observe are substantially larger than per-rep measurement noise. First-query improvements range from 30\% to 90\%, and end-to-end latency differences span an order of magnitude between strategies. We therefore do not rely on marginal statistical significance for our headline conclusions. For differences that fall within the noise floor, we do not claim rankings. For example, the first-query latency of strategy \texttt{Dump} (a cache-dump strategy defined in Section~\ref{sec:strategy}) differs by less than 3 \textmu s across the three layouts, which is below the per-measurement noise level; we therefore conclude that \texttt{Dump} is layout-agnostic rather than ranking the layouts.

To quantify whether our conclusions generalize beyond a single workload instantiation, we perform a 10-seed workload sensitivity analysis. For each of Scattered-Zipf, Uniform-100K, and Tail-Mixed, we generate 10 independent random instantiations of the query stream (same database, same number of operations) and run the full measurement matrix on each seed. For each (workload, layout, strategy), we compute the per-seed effect as the percentage improvement of the strategy over its paired baseline within the same seed; this pairing cancels cross-seed machine-state drift. We then compute a bootstrap 95\% confidence interval for the mean effect using 10,000 percentile-method resamples. We additionally require sign consistency as a cross-check: we count how many of the 10 seeds show an improvement (negative effect). With 9 or more seeds agreeing in sign, this is approximately equivalent to a sign test with $p < 0.05$.

We acknowledge a statistical limitation: with n=10 seeds, the percentile bootstrap CI may under-cover at the tails; we therefore use sign consistency as a distribution-free cross-validation. We classify an effect as \textit{robust} if its CI excludes zero; \textit{directional} if the CI crosses zero but at least 7 of 10 seeds agree in sign; and \textit{tie} otherwise (effects that fall within the noise and are not reliably reproducible). These three classifications are used throughout Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} to distinguish reliable findings from incidental ones.

As a stability anchor, strategy \texttt{Dump} loads the entire resident working set; its first-query latency is independent of which key is accessed first. Across the 10-seed sweep, \texttt{Dump} first-query latency remains within 125.9 to 130.2 \textmu s, demonstrating that the sweep runs on a stable machine with no CPU throttling and that cross-seed variation arises from workload sampling rather than machine drift. Consequently, whenever a single-workload point estimate conflicts with the cross-seed CI, we defer to the cross-seed CI as the more reliable indicator.

\section{Prefetch Strategy Design}\label{sec:strategy}

\subsection{Two-Lever Selection: A Mandatory Interior Skeleton and a Conditional Leaf Bonus}\label{sec:dual-lever}

After cache clearing and before the first query executes, our framework issues \texttt{madvise(MADV\_WILLNEED)} hints for a selected set of pages. The delivery mechanism, whether asynchronous hints or synchronous \texttt{pread}, is orthogonal to the selection logic and was isolated in Section~\ref{sec:selection-delivery}. What distinguishes our strategies is solely which pages are selected for prefetching.

We organize the strategy space around two independent selection levers. The first lever, \textit{page-type awareness}, exploits the structural invariant of SQLite's B+tree: interior pages are few in number, universally required for any query, and together constitute only 368 KB (0.35\%) of the database. Rather than predicting future accesses from past behavior, we deduce that any query must traverse the interior skeleton, so prefetching these pages eliminates the dominant source of cold-start page faults. The second lever, \textit{access-frequency awareness}, observes which pages are actually resident after a workload warmup via \texttt{mincore} and prioritizes the most frequently accessed leaves. These two levers are complementary: page-type awareness provides coverage for uniform workloads with no natural hot set, while access-frequency awareness unlocks additional gains on skewed workloads where a small number of leaves dominate accesses.

The page-type lever encompasses the \texttt{Skel-N} family, the range and per-page variants (\texttt{Skel-all-range} and \texttt{Skel-all-page}), and the interior component of \texttt{Skel}. A critical clarification is that ``first $N$ interiors by file offset'' equates to ``the top $N$ levels of the B+tree'' only when the database layout is type-aware, that is, when all interior pages are physically clustered at the file head. Under the default SQLite layout (\texttt{Default}), where interiors and leaves are interleaved, \texttt{Skel-N} simply prefetches the earliest $N$ interior pages in the file, which may bear no relation to tree depth. This distinction explains the workload-dependent behavior we quantify in Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}. The \texttt{Skel} strategy improves on \texttt{Skel-N} by prefetching only the interior pages that are actually resident after a workload warmup, determined via \texttt{mincore}. For Tail-Mixed, for example, \texttt{Skel} prefetches only 4 interior pages rather than all 92, as the query stream touches a narrow right-edge navigation path of the B+tree. Note that \texttt{Skel} uses residency observation to \emph{filter} the interior set to those actually used; it does not rank pages by frequency. The frequency lever enters only through the leaf component of \texttt{Skel+K}.

The access-frequency lever extends \texttt{Skel} by adding the most frequently accessed leaves. For a given workload, we run a warmup pass, record per-page access counts, and select the top $K$ leaves. The \texttt{Skel+K} strategies thus combine structural coverage, the interior skeleton, with workload-specific hot data, the high-frequency leaves. We sweep $K \in \{10, 50, 100, 500\}$ in Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}; the sweep itself illustrates the cost-accounting trade-off, as larger $K$ improves first-query latency but inflates delivery cost.

The \texttt{Dump} strategy serves as a contrasting extreme. It captures the full resident working set after a workload warmup, mimicking the application-side analogue of InnoDB's buffer pool dump and load. This strategy prefetches the entire working set, approximately 4,400 pages (17.7 MB) for Scattered-Zipf and Uniform-100K, and represents the ``prefetch everything'' baseline against which our frugal targeted strategies are compared. Its behavior in end-to-end latency, detailed in Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}, reveals why larger hotsets do not necessarily yield faster cold starts.

All access-pattern-based hotsets (\texttt{Skel}, \texttt{Skel+K}, and \texttt{Dump}) are generated offline by a separate pipeline: a cold cache clear, a workload warmup, and a \texttt{mincore} snapshot. As noted in Section~\ref{sec:cost-model}, this is a one-time or periodic cost that is amortized across repeated cold starts and does not appear in the per-event critical-path end-to-end latency. The hotset checksum is frozen and verified by the harness before each measurement to ensure reproducibility.

\subsection{Physical Layout Rewriting}\label{sec:layout}

The physical placement of pages within the SQLite file determines whether sequential prefetch hints can efficiently cover multiple interior pages in a single I/O operation. We evaluate three layout variants that share the same logical database content but differ in page arrangement.

The default layout, \texttt{Default}, is produced when the database is populated through standard SQLite inserts. Interior and leaf pages are interleaved throughout the file, with a scatter score of 0.96. We define the scatter score as a normalized measure of interior-page dispersion across the file, where 0 indicates perfect clustering and higher values indicate greater fragmentation. The \texttt{Vacuum} layout is generated by invoking SQLite's built-in \texttt{VACUUM} command, which rewrites the database file in insertion order but does not consider page types. In our experiments, \texttt{VACUUM} increases the scatter score to 1.13, making the layout more fragmented rather than less, and reduces the file size by approximately 3\%. This outcome establishes that \texttt{VACUUM} is not a solution to the interior-page scattering problem.

The \texttt{Clustered} layout is produced by our custom binary-level layout rewriter. The rewriter relocates all 92 interior pages to the beginning of the file, placing them contiguously in pages 2 through 93, followed by leaf pages, with freelist and overflow pages appended at the end. The rewriter patches every cross-page pointer affected by this relocation: interior page child pointers, overflow page next-page pointers, freelist next-trunk pointers, and the freelist pointer in the page 1 header. It also emits SQL to update the \texttt{rootpage} fields in \texttt{sqlite\_master}. The resulting layout achieves a scatter score of 0.0001, clustering all interior pages within the first 400 KB of the file. The rewriter passes \texttt{PRAGMA integrity\_check} on the rewritten database, confirming that all pointer references remain valid.

Figure~\ref{fig:layout-distribution} visualizes the physical distribution of the 92 interior pages under the three layouts. The contrast between the scattered default layout and the tightly clustered type-aware layout motivates the design of \texttt{Clustered}: with interior pages confined to the first 400 KB, a single range-based prefetch hint can cover the entire critical set, potentially amortizing the cold-start I/O cost into a single sequential read. We evaluate this potential in Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} and, as we preview below, find that it does not translate into end-to-end wins under our workloads.

It is important to be precise about the role of \texttt{Clustered} in our framework. Clustering interior pages at the file head is a double-edged sword. The benefit is that it enables structural prefetch strategies, such as \texttt{Skel-N}, to cover the critical interior set with a single range hint. The cost is that relocating interiors to the file head pushes leaf pages to higher offsets, which raises the no-prefetch baseline for workloads that are dominated by cold leaf faults. (A production system, \texttt{sqlite\_web\_vfs}'s \texttt{.dbi} side file~\cite{SqliteWebVfs}, sidesteps this displacement by copying navigation-critical pages rather than reordering them in place; we return to this in Section~\ref{sec:eval-attribution}.) In our Scattered-Zipf/Uniform-100K/Tail-Mixed workload matrix, the best end-to-end performance consistently occurs with the default layout (\texttt{Default}) combined with access-pattern prefetching (\texttt{Skel} or \texttt{Skel+K}); \texttt{Clustered} does not outperform \texttt{Default} in any measured cell. The type-aware layout is therefore a negative result under our workloads, useful only in the narrow scenario where prefetching will definitely be deployed, structural targeting is the only available mechanism (for example, when no access-pattern observation is possible), and the workload is sufficiently skewed that the interior prefetch gain outweighs the leaf-offset penalty. We provide the full per-cell comparison and the precise net-win conditions in Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}. This honest assessment aligns with the framing in Section~\ref{sec:contribution}, where we introduced the layout rewriter as a design alternative we evaluated and found to be negative, rather than as a recommended deployment default.

\subsection{Deployment Integration}\label{sec:deployment}

The cost model in Section~\ref{sec:cost-model} distinguished two deployment scenarios at the level of end-to-end latency accounting. Here we describe their implementation. In the standalone warmer deployment, prefetching runs as a separate process, typically a sidecar, that must cold-open the database file independently. This corresponds to Eq.~\ref{eq:e2e_std}, where the cold open cost $t_{\text{open}}$ is paid on every invocation. In the integrated deployment, the application itself issues prefetch hints after its SQLite handle is already open and before serving the first request. This corresponds to Eq.~\ref{eq:e2e_warm}, where the handle is reused and $t_{\text{open}}$ is not paid. The integrated deployment is the model we advocate for serverless keep-alive and edge worker scenarios, where the function runtime remains resident across invocations.

For multi-process deployments, such as multiple function instances on the same node sharing a database file, our framework leverages Linux's \texttt{mmap(MAP\_SHARED)} to share a single OS page cache across all processes. With SQLite's \texttt{PRAGMA mmap\_size} set to the file size, the database file is mapped into memory with \texttt{MAP\_SHARED}; pages brought into the cache by one process become visible to all others that share the same mapping. A single prefetch operation thus benefits every process accessing the database. A private-cache configuration, where each process maintains its own buffer via standard \texttt{read()} calls, serves as a baseline for comparison. The shared-memory advantage, along with the trade-off between prefetch cadence and cache freshness, is evaluated in the cadence experiments of Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}.

\section{Experimental Setup}\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Platform}\label{sec:platform}

All experiments run on a single bare-metal commodity machine: an AMD Ryzen 9950X CPU with 64 GB of physical memory (62 GiB available) and a KINGSTON SKC3000D2048G (2 TB, NVMe Gen4 $\times$4) SSD. The operating system is Linux kernel 6.17. The kernel readahead window, \texttt{read\_ahead\_kb}, is fixed at its system default of 128 KB. We do not sweep this parameter (see Section~\ref{sec:selection-delivery}). Frequency pinning follows the protocol of Section~\ref{sec:protocol}. All experiments are conducted on this single machine and kernel; we discuss the scope and limitations of this platform in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

\subsection{Reference Database}\label{sec:refdb}

All experiments use a fixed reference database with a single table, \texttt{items(id INTEGER PRIMARY KEY, k1 INTEGER, k2 INTEGER, payload BLOB(100))}, and a secondary index on \texttt{(k1, k2)}. The database contains 600,000 rows, occupies approximately 102 MB, and uses 4 KB pages. The page composition, including the 92 interior pages and 26,239 leaf pages, is detailed in Section~\ref{sec:btree}. Three layout variants share the same logical content but differ in physical page arrangement: the default layout (\texttt{Default}) and the type-aware layout (\texttt{Clustered}) each contain 26,331 pages; the \texttt{VACUUM} layout (\texttt{Vacuum}) contains 25,613 pages. A 10$\times$ scaled variant with 6,000,000 rows and 263,991 pages ($\sim$1 GiB) is used in the size-scaling experiments (Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}).

\subsection{Workloads}\label{sec:workloads}

We evaluate three measured read workloads that cover orthogonal dimensions of access-pattern variation. Scattered-Zipf is read-only point queries from a scrambled Zipfian distribution over a 100K-key active domain. Uniform-100K is uniform read-only point queries over the same 100K-key active domain, used as a matched no-skew control. Tail-Mixed is a 20K-key interval crossing the database maximum, producing approximately 50\% successful and 50\% not-found queries. Alongside these we run one database-evolution schedule, Mixed-Mutation Churn, used only to age the database between measured probes and not latency-measured. We classify the workloads into three roles. Scattered-Zipf, Uniform-100K, Tail-Mixed, and the Tail-Hit control are controlled read workloads. Latest-Aging and Short-Scan Aging are deterministic Python reconstructions of YCSB-D and YCSB-E semantics. Mixed-Mutation Churn is a mutation schedule, not a measured workload. We refer to every workload by its descriptive name throughout, which also avoids confusion with the standard YCSB core workloads A--F.

Scattered-Zipf, Uniform-100K, and Tail-Mixed are designed to represent three distinct access characteristics: skew (Scattered-Zipf), uniformity (Uniform-100K), and mixed tail-boundary lookups (Tail-Mixed). Scattered-Zipf follows the access-distribution profile of YCSB-C~\cite{Cooper2010} (read-only Zipfian, $\alpha = 0.99$), with a key permutation that scatters hot keys across the 100{,}000-key active domain, analogous to YCSB's scrambled Zipfian; our generator reproduces the distribution rather than YCSB's exact sampling implementation. Both Scattered-Zipf and Uniform-100K follow the YCSB op-string format (\texttt{read <key>}, \texttt{update <key>}, \texttt{scan <key> <len>})\footnote{\url{https://github.com/ls4154/YCSB-cpp}}. Tail-Mixed and Mixed-Mutation Churn are custom designs specific to this study; they have no YCSB equivalent. Scattered-Zipf and Uniform-100K draw keys from a 100,000-key active domain (ids 1 through 100,000), which lies entirely within the database's 600,000-key extent. Tail-Mixed instead draws uniformly over a 20,000-key interval (ids 590,000 through 609,999) that crosses the database maximum id of 600,000, so roughly half of its lookups fall beyond the maximum and become not-found probes. Each read workload executes 100,000 operations.

Three auxiliary robustness workloads support specific controls, all on the original layout (\texttt{Default}) only. Tail-Hit is a matched pure-hit control that draws the same-size tail range entirely within the existing key space (zero not-found), and it isolates the not-found effect in the attribution analysis of Section~\ref{sec:eval-attribution}. Latest-Aging (read-latest) and Short-Scan Aging (short-ranges) are deterministic Python reconstructions of the YCSB-D and YCSB-E core semantics rather than official YCSB traces, and they drive the static-plan staleness experiment of Section~\ref{sec:eval-robustness}: Latest-Aging's read hotspot moves over time (non-stationary), whereas Short-Scan Aging's is stationary. Neither Tail-Hit nor Latest-Aging/Short-Scan Aging alters the Scattered-Zipf/Uniform-100K/Tail-Mixed first-query baselines. Hashed-Hotspot is a native YCSB hotspot read workload (hot-data-fraction $1\%$) whose hot keys are hashed across the physical key space, so the hot leaves are scattered rather than contiguous; it is used only in the cross-platform portability analysis of Section~\ref{sec:eval-portability} and is not equated with Tail-Mixed.

The three read workloads each produce the cold first-query baselines reported throughout this paper: under the default layout (\texttt{Default}), the median cold first-query latencies are 523~\textmu s for Scattered-Zipf, 749~\textmu s for Uniform-100K, and 1087~\textmu s for Tail-Mixed. These baseline values are used as the denominator for all improvement calculations in Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}. The original workload traces are published with our artifact (checksummed); the accompanying generator reproduces their distributions and drives the 10-seed sensitivity analysis of Section~\ref{sec:stats}.

\subsection{OpenWhisk Portability Deployment}\label{sec:setup-openwhisk}

The cross-platform portability analysis (Section~\ref{sec:eval-portability})
runs the same prefetch toolchain inside a genuine container-based FaaS runtime,
Apache OpenWhisk, on the same bare-metal host described above; no hardware or
kernel changes are introduced, so the deployment isolates the container-runtime
and deployment dimension rather than a new hardware configuration. The action
loads an identical bound reference database---the original-layout (\texttt{Default})
\texttt{test.db} of 26{,}331 pages---and issues prefetch hints through the same
non-privileged application-layer interfaces (\texttt{mmap}, \texttt{madvise})
used on the workstation (Contribution~3); nothing in the container requires
kernel, FTL, or runtime modification.

Each measured invocation is one arm of an A/B pair: a same-condition baseline
arm and a strategy arm, scheduled under a block-union paired design with
position balancing. We report the warm paired first-query, which is a
deployment-feasibility and relative-effectiveness signal rather than an absolute
strategy-performance estimate; absolute microseconds are never compared between
OpenWhisk and the workstation. The comparison quantity is the relative
first-query reduction $R$ defined in Section~\ref{sec:eval-portability}.

The OpenWhisk evidence accumulates over seven immutable, additively-frozen
deployment campaigns totalling 5{,}756 invocations (2{,}878 A/B pairs). The
campaigns answer distinct coverage questions and are accounted \emph{without
pooling}: the totals are bookkeeping across the seven frozen campaigns, never a
single pooled effect estimate. Together they give complete coverage of the
strategy$\times$workload cells the workstation ran---65 of 65 (55 first-query
cells plus 10 delivery-order \texttt{LP} cells).

\section{Evaluation}\label{sec:evaluation}

\subsection{First-Query Gains and Workload-Dependent Ceilings}\label{sec:eval-fq}

We begin by establishing the first-query latency improvements achieved by each strategy, before accounting for prefetch preprocessing in Section~\ref{sec:eval-tradeoff}. Under a cold OS page cache, the baseline first-query latencies are 523 \textmu s for Scattered-Zipf, 749 \textmu s for Uniform-100K, and 1087 \textmu s for Tail-Mixed (Section~\ref{sec:workloads}). The cache-dump strategy \texttt{Dump} achieves the lowest first-query latency across all three workloads: 108 \textmu s for Scattered-Zipf ($-79\%$), 107 \textmu s for Uniform-100K ($-86\%$), and 102 \textmu s for Tail-Mixed ($-91\%$). If the evaluation stopped at first-query latency, \texttt{Dump} would be the unambiguous winner. The targeted strategies achieve reductions ranging from $-27\%$ to $-83\%$, with the specific ceiling depending on the workload.

Table~\ref{tab:ceiling} summarizes the workload-dependent benefit ceiling for first-query improvement. Scattered-Zipf, which exhibits a skewed Zipfian access pattern, concentrates first-query probability on a small set of leaves (all pages are nonetheless cold at measurement time); prefetching interior pages alone captures the dominant benefit, yielding $-27\%$ to $-30\%$ improvement. Adding more hot leaves through \texttt{Skel+500} reaches $-64\%$, and the full cache dump reaches $-79\%$, but the marginal gain of adding leaves beyond the interior skeleton is modest. Uniform-100K, which accesses keys uniformly at random, has no natural hot set; every query touches a cold leaf, and interior-only prefetching is capped at approximately $-44\%$. Only the full cache dump, which loads the entire resident working set, reduces first-query latency further to $-86\%$. Tail-Mixed, which reads a high-key tail range roughly half of which lies beyond the database's maximum key, faces cold leaves on every query; the access-pattern strategy \texttt{Skel+10} breaks through the interior-only ceiling of $-39\%$ largely because the $\sim$50\% not-found probes concentrate on the rightmost leaf, which \texttt{Skel+10} prefetches, achieving $-83\%$ first-query reduction (a key-range artifact, not general tail prediction; Section~\ref{sec:eval-attribution}).

The effectiveness of structural prefetching, \texttt{Skel-N}, depends on both the workload and the physical layout. On Scattered-Zipf, prefetching the first five interior pages reaches a plateau at approximately $-27\%$ to $-30\%$ first-query reduction; additional interior pages beyond $N=5$ provide no further benefit. Uniform-100K exhibits the same pattern: $N=5$ is already sufficient, and the plateau sits at approximately $-44\%$. Tail-Mixed, in contrast, requires nearly all 92 interior pages ($N \approx 92$): \texttt{Skel-N} selects interiors by file offset, while the interiors covering Tail-Mixed's high-key tail range sit mid-file, so offset-order selection misses them until $N$ spans nearly the full set. Prefetching only a single interior page ($N=1$) is counter-productive on Scattered-Zipf: the \texttt{madvise} dispatch overhead exceeds the coverage benefit, making first-query latency approximately $+31\%$ worse than the same-batch baseline; on Uniform-100K and Tail-Mixed, $N=1$ is slightly better than the baseline by a few percent. The mechanism behind the \texttt{Skel-N} plateau is subtle. At first-query time, both interior and leaf pages are equally cold; prefetching interior pages removes interior faults but leaves the first leaf fault intact. After the query stream warms up, the hot leaves corresponding to frequently accessed keys become naturally resident in the cache. The interior pages, however, remain the only shared bottleneck across all queries. Prefetching the interior skeleton is therefore sufficient for workloads with stable hot keys, while workloads whose first queries strike cold leaves require more: Uniform-100K, with no natural hot set, is served only by the full dump, whereas Tail-Mixed's concentrated tail region makes access-frequency targeting effective. The effect of the hot key location, such as low-id hotspots, is secondary; the shape of the N-sweep is consistent across hotspot variants in our artifact.

\subsection{Attribution: Ablation and Competitive Baselines}\label{sec:eval-attribution}

The large single-instantiation result of Section~\ref{sec:eval-tradeoff} on Tail-Mixed combines two selection levers: page-type awareness (interior pages) and access-frequency awareness (hot leaves). We isolate their individual contributions using an exact decomposition of the \texttt{Skel+10} hotset. This attribution required correcting a subtle estimator artifact. Our original hot-leaf ranking broke frequency ties by first-seen order; on a tied-count workload this preferentially selected the earliest-observed leaves, which coincide with the measured first query and leak into the hotset. We replaced it with a deterministic, trace-order-independent tie-break (frequency descending, then page number ascending) and re-measured the affected cells in a same-batch rerun; the corrected numbers below reverse the pre-fix attribution:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{Skel}: page-type lever only; prefetches resident interiors after workload warmup.
    \item \texttt{leaf\_freq\_K10}: access-frequency lever only; prefetches the top-10 most frequently accessed leaves.
    \item \texttt{leaf\_rand\_K10}: control group; prefetches 10 leaves of the same type (leaf\_table) drawn uniformly at random, controlling for page count and type while isolating the effect of frequency ranking.
    \item \texttt{Skel+10}: union of \texttt{Skel} and \texttt{leaf\_freq\_K10}; exact decomposition.
\end{itemize}

The control group is the crucial design element: \texttt{leaf\_rand\_K10} and \texttt{leaf\_freq\_K10} have the same page count (10) and the same page type (leaf), differing only in whether selection is frequency-based or random. Any difference between them is attributable purely to access-frequency awareness.

The corrected ablation and competitive-baseline experiments run as a single same-batch 10-seed rerun with paired baselines; cross-seed means therefore differ from the single-instantiation numbers of Sections~\ref{sec:eval-fq} and~\ref{sec:eval-tradeoff}. Table~\ref{tab:ablation} presents the cross-seed means for Tail-Mixed. The interior-only lever, \texttt{Skel}, is $-43\%$ first-query and $-36\%$ end-to-end, and is the only \emph{robust} contributor (CI excludes zero). The random-leaf control is net-harmful in end-to-end ($+7\%$), showing that loading extra leaves without a real hotspot costs deliver time for no coverage. Crucially, the frequency-selected leaves alone, \texttt{leaf\_freq\_K10}, are only $-3\%$ end-to-end with a confidence interval that crosses zero, i.e.\ a tie: their pre-fix value of $-40\%$ was almost entirely first-op leakage. The combined strategy, \texttt{Skel+10}, reaches $-63\%$ first-query and $-55\%$ end-to-end, but is bimodal across first-op hit/miss seeds (discussed below). The attribution therefore reverses relative to the pre-fix analysis: page-type (interior) awareness is the robust lever and the enabler of any leaf benefit (a leaf is useless while its interior path is uncached), whereas leaf-frequency alone is not a robust standalone signal on this workload.

Changing the physical layout from the default (\texttt{Default}) to the type-aware layout (\texttt{Clustered}) does not change the selection story: it increases the number of interior pages prefetched by \texttt{Skel} on Tail-Mixed from 4 to 48, but the warm e2e improvement is slightly worse ($-63\%$ vs $-75\%$ for \texttt{Default}), consistent with the layout negative result we detail below.

The corrected ablation establishes an asymmetric hierarchy, not two equal levers. Page-type awareness is the robust default: it carries Uniform-100K, supplies the interior skeleton for Scattered-Zipf and Tail-Mixed, and is the prerequisite for any leaf benefit. Access-frequency leaf selection is a conditional add-on: it contributes a genuine, robust bonus only where a real hotspot exists (the Zipfian skew of Scattered-Zipf), and is a tie on the uniform-hit portion of Tail-Mixed. What looked, before the tie-break fix, like a large access-frequency contribution on Tail-Mixed was largely first-op leakage; the residual leaf signal on Tail-Mixed stems from its not-found rightmost-leaf concentration, discussed next, not from general leaf predictability.

The reason Tail-Mixed produces a large but narrow result is a property of its key range rather than of tail reads in general. Tail-Mixed draws uniformly over a high-key range whose upper half exceeds the reference database's maximum key, so approximately half of its lookups are not-found probes; every such probe descends the B+tree right edge to the same rightmost leaf, which therefore becomes an extreme, key-range-induced hotspot. Consequently Tail-Mixed's corrected \texttt{Skel+10} result is bimodal across seeds: when the measured first query is itself a not-found probe it hits this hotspot ($\approx -70\%$), and when it is a genuine hit it falls back to the interior-skeleton regime ($\approx -31\%$), for a cross-seed mean of $-55\%$. To confirm that this is a not-found artifact rather than a general uniform-tail-read benefit, we run a matched pure-hit control on the original layout, Tail-Hit, which draws the same-size tail range entirely within the existing key space (zero not-found). On this control the corrected \texttt{Skel+10} returns to $\approx -27\%$, statistically matching interior-only \texttt{Skel} ($-28.5\%$) and a footprint-matched frequency dump ($-30.6\%$): once the not-found concentration is removed, leaf-frequency selection adds essentially nothing beyond the interior skeleton. We report the large Tail-Mixed number therefore as a scoped, mechanism-specific result, not as the paper's general finding.

We next ask whether the cache-dump strategies used in Section~\ref{sec:eval-tradeoff}, specifically \texttt{Dump}, are an unfairly weak baseline. To address this concern, we design two competitive baselines that port the page-selection core of representative prior systems onto our common substrate, SQLite over the OS page cache, so that they can be evaluated on the cold-start critical path under the same cost accounting. \texttt{Dump} ports the full-working-set dump-and-reload core of InnoDB's buffer pool dump/load: it captures the entire resident working set (approximately 4,400 pages, 17.7 MB on Scattered-Zipf/Uniform-100K; 1.8 MB on Tail-Mixed) and reloads it at cold start. \texttt{Dump-N} implements a frequency-ranked dump in the spirit of Pre-Buffer's hot-page selection~\cite{Yi2026}: our variant replays each query's root-to-leaf traversal, counts page visits, and dumps the top-$N$ ranked pages ($N \in \{14, 28, 100, 500\}$). InnoDB's partial dump ranks by LRU recency, whereas Pre-Buffer ranks by access frequency; our arm implements the frequency variant. We deliberately reproduce only the selection mechanism, not the surrounding orchestration (InnoDB's startup-time reload and Pre-Buffer's background prefetch thread, Jaccard-similarity workload-change detection, dedicated prefetch buffer, and page-cache-bypassing Direct I/O), because that orchestration runs off the critical path and is orthogonal to our question. Neither prior system accounts for prefetch delivery cost on the user-facing critical path. By porting their selection cores into our cost-accounted first-query harness, we show that frequency-ranked selection at matched footprint ties our targeted strategy on both broad and narrow workloads, while the full-working-set dump, the InnoDB core, regresses warm-process end-to-end latency by up to $+762\%$ (cross-seed mean, as we show below). We sweep $N \in \{14, 28, 100, 500, \text{full}\}$.

Table~\ref{tab:competitive} shows the e2e warm results for a representative subset: the targeted strategy \texttt{Skel+10}, the tuned dump at its sweet spot (\texttt{Dump-14}), a larger tuned dump (\texttt{Dump-500}), and the full dump (\texttt{Dump}). Three conclusions follow. First, end-to-end performance monotonically worsens with dump footprint: full dump regresses by $+762\%$ (cross-seed mean) on Scattered-Zipf, compared to the single-instantiation figure of $+1300\%$ in Table~\ref{tab:e2e-ac}; and by $+730\%$ on Uniform-100K, demonstrating that the deliver cost of large hotsets is the fundamental problem, not the dump mechanism itself. Only small, frequency-ranked partial dumps win, with sweet spots at $N \approx 14$ to 28. Second, on the broad workloads Scattered-Zipf and Uniform-100K, \texttt{Dump-N} with matched footprint effectively matches \texttt{Skel+10}: Scattered-Zipf $-37\%$ (\texttt{Dump-28}) vs $-38\%$ (\texttt{Skel+10}); Uniform-100K $-27\%$ (\texttt{Dump-14}) vs $-24\%$ (\texttt{Skel+10}); CIs overlap. Page-type knowledge is not necessary for these workloads; frequency ranking alone suffices. Third, on the narrow workload Tail-Mixed, the corrected same-batch comparison shows \emph{no} advantage for explicit type awareness: \texttt{Skel+10} achieves $-55\%$ (CI $[-67, -42]$) and matched \texttt{Dump-14} achieves $-55\%$ (CI $[-67, -43]$), with essentially fully overlapping CIs; the two are statistically indistinguishable. The pre-fix appearance of a robust $-72\%$ vs $-57\%$ gap was an artifact of the first-op tie-break leakage. This is consistent with the ablation: because Tail-Mixed's leaf signal comes from a not-found rightmost-leaf concentration that a frequency-ranked dump also captures, footprint-matched frequency ranking recovers a similar page set. Explicit page typing is an interpretable way to \emph{guarantee} interior-skeleton coverage, but it does not uniquely win on Tail-Mixed.

The broader lesson is that \texttt{Skel+10} ties, but does not beat, a footprint-matched frequency-ranked dump across all three workloads. What matters is a small footprint combined with sound page selection; explicit page typing and pure frequency ranking recover a similar page set. Page-type awareness earns its place as an \emph{interpretable} mechanism that \emph{guarantees} interior-skeleton coverage independent of the observed access sample, rather than as a technique that uniquely outperforms frequency ranking.

Two prior-art arms cited in Section~\ref{sec:related} remain, each isolating a dimension the competitive baselines above hold fixed.

\textbf{Delivery order (libprefetch).} The competitive baselines above vary the prefetch \emph{set} at a fixed delivery order; the core contribution of libprefetch~\cite{VanDeBogart2009} is the opposite, varying delivery \emph{order} (C-LOOK offset sorting) at a fixed set. We reproduce it as an ordering-only comparison: two arms deliver the identical $\sim$18\,MB working set (whose sorted-order page set is identical to \texttt{Dump}), one in ascending file-offset order (\texttt{LP-sorted}), one shuffled (\texttt{LP-shuf}). On NVMe the ordering penalty survives only in the deliver term: synchronous \texttt{pread} delivery is $10$ to $16\times$ slower shuffled than sorted ($15.6\times$ Scattered-Zipf, $15.1\times$ Uniform-100K, $10.5\times$ Tail-Mixed), hundreds of milliseconds of avoidable serialized transfer. First-query latency is essentially unchanged between the two orderings (a warm control: both arms deliver the same pages); the entire ordering effect falls in the deliver term, orders of magnitude larger than any first-query difference. The penalty is specific to synchronous \texttt{pread}, which serializes the transfer; asynchronous \texttt{fadvise} dispatch, which only hands the kernel a hint, shows no ordering effect (deliver ratio $\approx 1.0$ on all three workloads). Without a block-level trace we make no device-mechanism claim beyond this synchronous/asynchronous split. libprefetch is thus orthogonal to our set-selection contribution: a delivery-scheduling optimization whose only NVMe residue is a deliver-term cost that leaves first-query latency untouched.

\textbf{Learned page-transition prefetching (Chen lineage).} The second arm is a learned prefetcher in the lineage of Chen et al.~\cite{Chen2021}: a first-order Markov page-transition model that computes finite-horizon expected page visits from the B$^+$tree root and prefetches the top-$N$. We evaluate it under leave-one-seed-out (LOSO) cross-validation across the 10 workload seeds (train on nine, test on the held-out seed) so no test trace informs its own prefetch set. At matched footprint it reduces cold first-query latency by $-39.6\%$ (Scattered-Zipf), $-36.6\%$ (Uniform-100K), and $-65.4\%$ (Tail-Mixed), a $-37\%$ to $-69\%$ range across cells with every 95\% CI excluding zero, at $100\%$ delivery on verified-cold caches. It ties footprint-matched frequency ranking and targeted prefetch on Uniform-100K and Tail-Mixed (overlapping CIs) but is worse on Scattered-Zipf ($-40\%$ versus $-51\%$ for same-budget frequency-ranked and hot-leaf arms): the learned transition model does not beat plain frequency ranking where the access distribution is broad, and matches it where the distribution is concentrated. (An earlier single-fold analysis reported a more optimistic held-out figure; the honest LOSO mean supersedes it.) These two arms therefore reinforce the broader lesson: neither delivery-order scheduling nor a learned transition model outperforms a small, soundly-selected prefetch set.

We now return to the physical layout rewriter (\texttt{Clustered}) introduced in Section~\ref{sec:layout}. Our evaluation finds no scenario in which \texttt{Clustered} outperforms the default layout (\texttt{Default}) when combined with access-pattern prefetching. The best warm e2e on each workload is achieved with \texttt{Default} plus access-pattern targeting: Scattered-Zipf 452 \textmu s vs \texttt{Clustered} 523 \textmu s, Uniform-100K 509 \textmu s vs \texttt{Clustered} 701 \textmu s, and (from the tie-break-corrected batch) Tail-Mixed \texttt{Skel+10} 265 \textmu s vs \texttt{Clustered} 317 \textmu s. Each orig-vs-\texttt{Clustered} pair is same-batch; the Tail-Mixed pair uses the corrected \texttt{tiebreak\_fix} values (orig $-75\%$, \texttt{Clustered} $-63\%$). The type-aware layout produces slightly larger percentage improvements for structural prefetching (on Scattered-Zipf, \texttt{Skel-92} first-query goes from $-30\%$ to $-32\%$), but this is an artifact: the pread first-query floor under \texttt{Clustered} is 187 \textmu s versus 187 \textmu s under \texttt{Default}; the larger percentage stems entirely from a raised baseline (523 to 658 \textmu s, $+26\%$), not from a lower absolute latency. Clustering interiors at the file head pushes leaves to higher offsets, raising the cold-leaf-fault baseline; the absolute benefit of interior prefetching does not improve. Notably, a production system arrives at the same conclusion from the
opposite direction: the \texttt{.dbi} side file (Section~\ref{sec:layout}) avoids in-place reordering entirely, sidestepping precisely the displacement penalty we quantify here. The narrow conditions under which \texttt{Clustered} could be net-positive, (i) prefetching is definitely deployed, (ii) only structural prefetching is available (no access-pattern observation), and (iii) the workload is sufficiently skewed, are not simultaneously met in any of our Scattered-Zipf/Uniform-100K/Tail-Mixed cells. We therefore recommend the default layout (\texttt{Default}) for production use, and treat \texttt{Clustered} as a negative result under our evaluated workloads.

\subsection{Robustness}\label{sec:eval-robustness}

We validate the headline conclusions across six robustness axes. All results in this section use the cross-seed classifications defined in Section~\ref{sec:stats}: \textit{robust} (95\% CI excludes zero), \textit{directional} (CI crosses zero but at least 7 of 10 seeds agree in sign), and \textit{tie} (effects within the noise). Each robustness axis is evaluated on a separate batch (distinct machine-state groups); comparisons are within-axis only. Absolute latencies may differ across axes, but the relative effects and verdicts are internally consistent.

\textbf{Workload instantiation sensitivity.} The single-workload numbers reported in Sections~\ref{sec:eval-fq} and~\ref{sec:eval-tradeoff} are point estimates from one instantiation of each workload. Three findings emerge. First, the robust, general result is the interior skeleton: \texttt{Skel} improves warm-process e2e by roughly $-25\%$ to $-30\%$ across Scattered-Zipf, Uniform-100K, and the pure-hit control (Scattered-Zipf $-25\%$, Uniform-100K $-25\%$, Tail-Hit $-28.5\%$), all with CIs excluding zero; the larger $-36\%$ belongs to Scattered-Zipf's \texttt{Skel+10} genuine-skew bonus and to Tail-Mixed's not-found-inflated \texttt{Skel}, not to the general skeleton. Second, Tail-Mixed \texttt{Skel+10}'s cross-seed mean is $-55\%$ $[-67, -43]$ but bimodal (Section~\ref{sec:eval-attribution}); its magnitude is not stable and reflects Tail-Mixed's not-found concentration rather than a general access-pattern win. The three workloads' best access-pattern strategies are all cross-seed improvements (Scattered-Zipf \texttt{Skel+10} $-36\%$ $[-50, -23]$, Uniform-100K \texttt{Skel} $-25\%$ $[-32, -16]$), but the robust, portable claim is the interior skeleton, not the Tail-Mixed headline number. Third, the structural heuristic \texttt{Skel-5} does not survive: on Scattered-Zipf it is \textit{tie} ($-5\%$, CI crosses zero, 6/10), and on Uniform-100K the verdict is \textit{directional}—toward regression: 8 of 10 seeds worsen (median $+5\%$), with the cross-seed mean pulled to $-1\%$ by two strongly improving seeds. The structural heuristic is not merely unstable but, in most instantiations, actively harmful. The robustness of the headline is carried entirely by access-pattern strategies; structural prefetching without access observation is unreliable.

The methodological lesson from the 10-seed sweep is that single-workload point estimates can be misleading. The main batch happened to draw a Scattered-Zipf instance whose first query was unusually cheap (baseline $\sim$520 \textmu s versus a cross-seed typical $\sim$900 \textmu s), leading to a cross-seed corrected improvement of $-36\%$ compared to the single-instantiation estimate of $-11\%$. Conversely, the same batch overestimated Uniform-100K \texttt{Skel-5} at $-34\%$, which is near zero in the cross-seed mean ($-1\%$). Both biases are only detectable with multi-seed sampling, validating the methodology of Section~\ref{sec:stats}.

\textbf{Memory pressure.} Serverless platforms enforce per-function memory caps via cgroup \texttt{MemoryMax}; when the cap falls below the working set, page reclaim competes with prefetch delivery. We sweep caps from 16 MB to 6 MB, representing 0.92$\times$ to 0.35$\times$ the working set for Scattered-Zipf and Uniform-100K. Figure~\ref{fig:ram} shows the result. The five targeted strategies (hotsets ranging from 20 KB to 2 MB) maintain 100\% delivery across all caps; their hotsets are orders of magnitude smaller than the memory cap, so reclaim never touches them. First-query latency remains flat. In contrast, \texttt{Dump}, whose hotset is the full working set (17.7 MB), loses delivery linearly as the cap shrinks (100\% to 20\%), and its first-query latency jumps directly from 98 \textmu s to the cold baseline of approximately 500 \textmu s as soon as delivery drops below 100\%. The cache-dump approach exhibits no graceful degradation; it is all-or-nothing. For Tail-Mixed, whose working set is only $\sim$1.8 MB, the cap cannot be meaningfully reduced below the working set without hitting the measurement floor; its RAM robustness is a deductive consequence of its hotset being smaller than any measurable cap and is not empirically tested in this axis.

\textbf{Write churn.} Serverless data stores ingest continuously between invocations; we therefore subject the database to 50,000 mutations (10 rounds of 5,000 writes) and measure the static $t=0$ hotset across 11 checkpoints. This churn batch predates the trace-order-independent tie-break correction; we therefore use it only to assess within-batch flatness across checkpoints, and treat the absolute level as diagnostic rather than as a corrected estimate of the \texttt{Skel+10} effect magnitude. Within the same pre-fix estimator and batch, the frozen plan does not show a systematic latency increase across checkpoints when the read hotspot remains stationary: on Tail-Mixed, \texttt{Skel+10} maintains $\sim$82--89 \textmu s across checkpoints (aside from one transient spike at checkpoint 4), compared to a baseline of $\sim$580 \textmu s (these absolute microseconds are pre-fix diagnostic values, not a corrected magnitude); Uniform-100K, which has no natural hot leaves, is similarly flat. Under this stationary-read-hotspot configuration, no plan refresh was needed to preserve the within-batch trend over 50k mutations. This scope is important---write churn perturbs page layout but leaves the \emph{read} hotspot in place; a read hotspot that moves over time is a distinct axis, the non-stationary Latest-Aging versus stationary Short-Scan Aging contrast examined next, under which a frozen frequency plan does decay. The corresponding figure is available in our artifact.

\textbf{Static-plan staleness under a moving hotspot.} We ask what happens when the read hotspot itself drifts, using two aging robustness workloads that are deterministic Python reconstructions of YCSB semantics (Section~\ref{sec:workloads}): Latest-Aging (read-latest), whose hotspot moves as new keys are inserted (non-stationary), and Short-Scan Aging (short-ranges), whose hotspot is stationary. A frozen $t{=}0$ plan is replayed across 11 checkpoints (10 seeds, bootstrap 95\% CI). Under Latest-Aging, the frozen \emph{frequency} plan (\texttt{Skel+10-static}) decays as the hotspot moves away from it: its warm first-query benefit erodes from $-50\%$ at checkpoint~0 to $-33\%$ at checkpoint~10, while the \emph{structural} plan (\texttt{Skel-92-static}), which covers the navigation skeleton independent of which keys are hot, holds at $-53\%$ across all checkpoints. Under the stationary Short-Scan Aging, both plans stay flat ($-53\%\!\to\!-55\%$ frequency, $-53\%\!\to\!-51\%$ structural). A static frequency plan is therefore safe only while the read hotspot is stationary; once it moves, structural coverage is the durable choice, matching the recommendation in Table~\ref{tab:guidance}. The corresponding figure is available in our artifact.

\textbf{Cadence and MAP\_SHARED.} With \texttt{mmap(MAP\_SHARED)}, a single prefetch operation benefits all processes sharing the same OS page cache. A background warmer re-warms the hotset at a configurable cadence. At cadence values of 1 s and 5 s, first-query latency is maintained at 26 \textmu s and 29 \textmu s, respectively; at 30 s or never, it regresses to 281--305 \textmu s, near the no-prefetch baseline. The cadence is a trade-off parameter: reducing cadence maintains warmth at the cost of additional I/O and CPU overhead; increasing cadence saves resources but allows the cache to cool. There is no single best cadence; the operator selects a point on the frontier based on the workload's query interval.

\textbf{Database size scaling.} We scale the reference database by $10\times$ to $\sim$1 GiB (6 million rows, 263,991 pages) and rerun the experiment on a separate batch. The first-query improvement is size-robust: across the 18 (workload $\times$ strategy) cells, all cells show the same direction of improvement as on 102 MB, and all 1 GiB cells are robust (CI excludes zero). The warm-process e2e sensitivity is concentrated on Tail-Mixed for dump-style strategies: \texttt{Dump} goes from $-9\%$ on 102 MB to $+139\%$ on 1 GiB, and \texttt{Skel+500} goes from $-31\%$ to $+35\%$, as the resident working set for Tail-Mixed expands from 483 to 984 pages, doubling the deliver cost. In contrast, the tie-break-unaffected interior skeleton \texttt{Skel} remains a robust win on Tail-Mixed at both sizes, and the size story is carried by the deliver-cost trend rather than by any corrected leaf-selector magnitude: the cache-dump deliver trap worsens with database size while frugal skeleton prefetch stays effective. (The pre-fix \texttt{Skel+10} figures of $-70\%$/$-68\%$ at 102~MB/1~GiB are diagnostic only; the corrected leaf-selector magnitude was not re-validated across sizes.) The corresponding figure is available in our artifact.

\subsection{Cross-Platform Effectiveness Portability}\label{sec:eval-portability}

The results so far were measured on a single workstation harness. To test
whether the \emph{relative} effectiveness ordering of strategies survives a
genuine container-based FaaS runtime, we replicated the strategy matrix on an
Apache OpenWhisk deployment (Section~\ref{sec:setup-openwhisk}) and compared it
to the workstation cell-by-cell.
Effectiveness is the relative first-query reduction against the same-condition
baseline, $R = (\mathit{fq}_{\text{base}} - \mathit{fq}_{\text{strat}}) /
\mathit{fq}_{\text{base}}$; only this relative quantity is comparable across
platforms, and we never compare absolute microseconds between the two. The
OpenWhisk evidence is accounted \emph{without pooling} across seven immutable
deployment campaigns (5{,}756 invocations / 2{,}878 A/B pairs); the warm paired
first-query is a deployment-feasibility and relative-effectiveness signal, not
an absolute strategy-performance estimate. This validation shares the
workstation's x86 and NVMe hardware, so it isolates the container-runtime and
deployment dimension rather than establishing hardware portability. Over the
strategy$\times$workload cells both platforms ran, coverage is complete: all 65
of 65 cells (55 first-query cells plus 10 delivery-order \texttt{LP} cells).

We classify each cell's effectiveness as effective ($R \geq 0.10$), harmful
($R \leq -0.10$), or neutral, and call a workstation cell strongly effective
when $R_{\text{ws}} \geq 0.30$. We report the per-cell rank correlation over
three nested subsets: all 55 first-query cells, a high-confidence subset of 46
(dropping low-sample cells), and a high-confidence \emph{clean} subset of 42
(also dropping the 4 position-sensitive cells whose baseline-first and
target-first subsets disagree in sign). Seven of the 55 first-query cells were
superseded, once and pre-registered, by exactly position-balanced replications;
the canonical historical table is retained byte-unchanged as the record.

Every strategy that is strongly effective on the workstation remains effective
under OpenWhisk: 41 of 41 strong-workstation first-query cells
(Figure~\ref{fig:portability}). The two
platforms' per-cell effectiveness ranks correlate at Spearman $\rho \approx
0.76$ over all 55 cells, $0.79$ over the high-confidence 46, and $0.81$ over the
clean 42. Per-cell effectiveness direction agrees in 46 of 55 cells, and the
median absolute effectiveness gap is $|\Delta R| \approx 0.11$.
Table~\ref{tab:portability} breaks the correlation down by workload; the ordering ports on
every skew regime, and the one weak correlation (Tail-Hit, $\rho \approx 0.25$)
is the pure-hit control whose cells are all effective on both platforms with

Three principles summarize the takeaways. First, the cache-dump strategy (\texttt{Dump}) minimizes first-query latency but is unsuitable for the cold-start critical path in all but the smallest working-set scenarios; its deliver overhead dominates response time, and its value lies in steady-state average latency rather than in user-facing first-query response. Moreover, even the small-working-set exception on Tail-Mixed erodes as the database grows (Section~\ref{sec:eval-robustness}), further reinforcing that cache-dump is not a robust critical-path solution. Second, the interior skeleton on the original layout (\texttt{Default} + \texttt{Skel}) is the recommended default for warm-process serverless deployments: it delivers consistent end-to-end improvements across all three workload types, survives cross-seed validation as robust, and has a memory footprint under 2 MB. A top-$K$ leaf budget (\texttt{Skel+10}) should be added \emph{only} when a stable access skew has been independently validated; and when the read hotspot moves over time, the durable choice is structural coverage or a periodic plan refresh rather than a frozen frequency plan. Third, the structural heuristic (\texttt{Skel-5}) should not be used alone. Its single-workload wins on Scattered-Zipf and Uniform-100K do not survive cross-seed validation; in most workload instantiations it is neutral or harmful. If structural prefetching is the only available mechanism, it should be paired with access-pattern observation or replaced by prefetching the full interior set ($N=92$, 368 KB), which does not depend on offset-order selection.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}\label{sec:discussion}

All experiments run on a single commodity x86 machine with a consumer-grade Gen4 NVMe SSD and a single Linux kernel version. Absolute microsecond latencies depend on the hardware, kernel, and runtime. The cost-accounting methodology is intended to transfer to platforms sharing the same warm-process, cold-data model. Whether the relative ordering of strategies carries over to a genuine FaaS runtime is an empirical question we test directly with an Apache OpenWhisk deployment (Section~\ref{sec:eval-portability}). Across every workstation--OpenWhisk cell we cover (65 of 65), the relative effectiveness ordering is preserved: every strategy that is strongly effective on the workstation (relative first-query reduction $R_{\text{ws}}\ge0.30$) is also effective under OpenWhisk (41 of 41 first-query cells), and the two platforms' per-cell effectiveness ranks correlate at Spearman $\rho\approx0.76$ to $0.81$. This is a relative-effectiveness result, accounted without pooling across seven immutable deployment campaigns; the OpenWhisk warm paired first-query is a deployment-feasibility and relative-effectiveness signal, not an absolute strategy-performance estimate. In particular, our measurements characterize the local-storage tier that production object-storage architectures interpose as a cache layer (e.g., Cloudflare D1's local-disk caching over R2~\cite{CloudflareD1}); the purely remote tier replaces the OS page cache with VFS-level range requests, changing the mechanism while preserving the dependent-fault-chain structure and multiplying per-fault cost by three to four orders of magnitude. This FaaS-runtime validation shares the same x86 and NVMe hardware as the primary evaluation, so it isolates the container-based runtime and deployment dimension rather than establishing hardware portability. A small number of per-cell disagreements (9 of 55 first-query cells) involve strategies that are neutral on at least one platform---including the single strategy that is harmful under OpenWhisk, \texttt{Skel-5} on Hashed-Hotspot, which is itself workstation-neutral and therefore not a regression of a workstation-effective strategy---and a few position-sensitive cells are reported as balanced-batch descriptive estimates reflecting pair-position and short-lived execution-storage state rather than clean causal effects. Portability across distinct hardware and to commercial FaaS providers remains future work.

Measurement batches exhibit session-to-session absolute drift of approximately 10\% to 15\%, attributable to SSD internal state and CPU thermal conditions. We monitor this drift using the \texttt{Dump} anchor, whose first-query latency is independent of the workload key distribution, and anchor all cross-strategy comparisons to same-batch baselines; cross-batch absolute comparisons are never made.

The workload coverage spans three distribution families, skew (Scattered-Zipf), uniformity (Uniform-100K), and mixed tail-boundary lookups (Tail-Mixed), with 10-seed sampling to cover within-family variation. It does not cover real-world mixed read-write traces or distribution families beyond these three. The RAM robustness of Tail-Mixed remains deductive rather than empirically measured, because its working set (approximately 1.8 MB) falls below the measurable cgroup pressure floor; constructing a larger working-set variant of Tail-Mixed is future work.

The measurement methodology stops at the OS page cache layer and cannot control SSD-internal FTL behavior, garbage collection, or wear leveling. The long-term evolution of page layout under sustained churn, beyond the 50k-mutation window, remains unobserved. A FEMU-based study is a natural extension.

The warm-process model differs from a full cold reboot by approximately 1 to 3 \textmu s of CPU cache warm-up bias, less than 1\% of the cold baseline. The strict cold-start mode, which quantifies this bias precisely, is left as future work.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

Serverless functions backed by SQLite experience a persistent cold-start bottleneck when the OS page cache is empty after idle periods, making the first query orders of magnitude slower than subsequent ones. Existing prefetching techniques focus on first-query latency without accounting for their own preprocessing overhead. We introduced a skeleton-first prefetch framework, in which prefetching the mandatory B+tree interior navigation path is the robust default and hot-leaf selection is a hotspot-conditional add-on, together with an end-to-end cost model that decomposes preprocessing into open and deliver terms under two deployment models. This paper demonstrates that first-query improvement does not guarantee end-to-end acceleration; the cache-dump strategy that minimizes first-query latency, \texttt{Dump}, regresses end-to-end cold starts by roughly an order of magnitude on broad workloads due to deliver overhead of 0.8 to 7 ms. In contrast, interior-only prefetch (\texttt{Skel}) achieves roughly $25$ to $30\%$ warm-process end-to-end improvement across the evaluated pure-hit workloads with a footprint under 2 MB, robust across seeds; adding hot leaves helps only under a verified concentrated hotspot (raising Scattered-Zipf from about $-25\%$ to $-36\%$ under genuine Zipfian skew), and a footprint-matched frequency-ranked dump is statistically indistinguishable from the type-aware selector. A mixed tail-boundary workload whose range is roughly half not-found high-key probes yields a larger but narrowly scoped, bimodal result driven by a rightmost-leaf hotspot; a matched pure-hit control returns to the skeleton regime. We additionally show that physical page reordering, a seemingly natural alternative, does not translate into end-to-end wins, and that the structural heuristic \texttt{Skel-5} is unreliable, falling into tie or directional-toward-regression under cross-seed validation. Consequently, no single strategy stack is universally optimal: the right choice is conditional on the deployment model and the workload's hotspot structure, with an interior-only skeleton on the original layout as the robust default. These conclusions are not confined to our measurement harness: on a genuine FaaS runtime (Apache OpenWhisk), the relative effectiveness ordering is preserved---every strongly-effective workstation strategy remains effective (41 of 41 first-query cells) and per-cell effectiveness ranks correlate at Spearman $\rho\approx0.76$ to $0.81$, over a full 65-of-65 cell coverage---showing that the strategy ordering, not merely the cost-accounting methodology, transfers to a container-based deployment.

To our knowledge, this is the first work to quantify the first-query versus end-to-end trade-off in SQLite-backed serverless cold starts at OS-syscall granularity. The framework is non-intrusive, operating entirely at the application layer with standard OS interfaces, and is designed to operate within the execution constraints of FaaS and edge environments.

For future work, the page-type knowledge that drives our selection lever can be pushed below the file abstraction: NVMe Stream Directives or ZNS can physically segregate interior and leaf pages at the SSD level, achieving type-aware placement without the leaf-displacement penalty that made file-level reordering a negative result, and preventing garbage collection from perturbing layout over time. In the object-storage regime, the same skeleton-first selection maps to a VFS-level question: which pages to coalesce into a single range request, and whether to issue background read-ahead. At millisecond per-fault costs, the trade-offs we quantify become correspondingly larger. A continuous prefetch direction, maintaining hot-set residency across invocations, would extend our cold-start framework to steady-state scenarios, but this would move the design point from low-frequency, no-root application-layer operation to the high-frequency, kernel-level regime occupied by Leis et al. \cite{Leis2023}, and would require re-evaluating the deployment model accordingly. More broadly, we argue that cost accounting, not first-query latency alone, should be the evaluation standard for cold-start prefetching.
