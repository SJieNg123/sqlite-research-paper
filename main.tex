\begin{abstract}
Serverless functions backed by SQLite face a persistent cold-start bottleneck: after idle periods the OS page cache is evicted, making the first query orders of magnitude slower than subsequent ones. Existing prefetching techniques optimize the post-prefetch first-query latency, implicitly assuming that improvement translates to a faster end-to-end cold start. We show that this assumption can mis-rank strategies, because the prefetch preprocessing itself sits on the user-visible critical path. Our primary contribution is a critical-path cost-accounting methodology: we decompose cold-start latency into a cold file open, per-page delivery, and the first query, evaluate it under two deployment boundaries (an integrated warm-process model that reuses an already-open handle and a standalone external-warmer model that pays the cold open), and separate page selection from page delivery. All operations use standard OS interfaces and require no modification to SQLite.

Once preprocessing is charged, the design rule that emerges is frugal coverage. A small set that covers the B+tree interior navigation path (footprint under 2 MB) yields a robust $25$ to $30\%$ warm-process end-to-end reduction across the evaluated pure-hit workloads. Hot-leaf prefetching is a conditional add-on that helps only under an independently verified concentrated hotspot, raising Scattered-Zipf from about $-25\%$ with interior coverage alone to about $-36\%$ under genuine Zipfian skew. At matched footprint, a frequency-ranked dump is statistically indistinguishable from the type-aware selector, so structural page-type knowledge earns its place as an interpretable coverage guarantee rather than a uniquely faster selector. The counterexample that motivates the metric is the full-working-set cache dump: it minimizes first-query latency ($-79\%$ to $-91\%$) yet regresses broad-workload end-to-end cold starts by about an order of magnitude, because its 0.8 to 7 ms delivery cost dominates the response, with the narrow small-working-set workload Tail-Mixed the sole exception. A mixed tail-boundary workload whose key range is roughly half not-found high-key lookups produces a larger but narrowly scoped, bimodal result driven by a rightmost-leaf hotspot. A matched pure-hit control returns to the $\approx-27\%$ to $-30\%$ coverage regime, and static frequency plans decay when the read hotspot moves whereas structural coverage remains stable. Finally, the first-query effectiveness signal remains broadly consistent on a genuine FaaS runtime: on a separate-host Apache OpenWhisk deployment, all 41 workstation cells with strong first-query effectiveness remain effective, and per-cell first-query effectiveness ranks correlate at Spearman $\rho\approx0.76$ to $0.81$. This is a first-query portability result, not a cross-platform end-to-end or hardware-portability claim. To our knowledge, this is the first SQLite OS-page-cache cold-start evaluation to separate open, delivery, and first-query terms and align them with integrated and standalone deployment boundaries.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

\subsection{Background}\label{sec:background}

\subsubsection{Serverless SQLite as a Growing Deployment Target}\label{sec:serverless-sqlite}

Serverless computing has become a main model of execution for cloud applications, offering transparent scaling, fine-grained billing, and simplified operational management. An increasing number of serverless workloads are stateful and require access to persistent data stored in embedded databases co-located with function instances. Among embedded databases, SQLite stands out as the world's most widely deployed database engine. Gaffney et al. \cite{Gaffney2022} estimate that over one trillion SQLite databases are in active use and that the engine is embedded in nearly every smartphone, sensor, automobile, and television. The same study identifies edge computing as a key emerging use case for SQLite, where lightweight zero-configuration data management is essential. FaaS platforms such as AWS Lambda, Azure Functions, and Google Cloud Functions allow functions to mount persistent storage or use ephemeral local storage for SQLite databases, making SQLite a natural choice for serverless applications that require function-local state, configuration, and lightweight analytics. In such deployments, SQLite serves as the storage layer that supports serverless functions, and its performance directly impacts the response time of the entire function. Production object-storage architectures interpose exactly this layer as a local cache tier: Cloudflare D1 caches object-stored databases on local disk to absorb query traffic~\cite{CloudflareD1}.

\subsubsection{The Warm Container, Cold Data Phenomenon}\label{sec:warm-container}

Wang et al. \cite{Wang2018} present the first large-scale measurement study of commercial serverless platforms, launching over 50,000 function instances across AWS Lambda, Azure Functions, and Google Cloud Functions. Their work reveals that serverless platforms rely on container or virtual machine reuse to manage resources efficiently, validating that the ``warm container'' state is a common deployment pattern in production. They also demonstrate that resource contention between co-located functions is common, providing a root cause for OS page cache eviction between invocations: when multiple functions compete for memory and I/O resources, the file-backed pages cached during one invocation may be reclaimed before the next invocation arrives. Shahrad et al. \cite{Shahrad2020} complement this analysis with a large-scale characterization of production FaaS workloads using Azure Functions trace data. Their study shows that invocation frequencies span eight orders of magnitude, with the vast majority of functions invoked infrequently, and that inter-invocation intervals are highly variable, making perfect prediction of future invocations impossible. To balance performance and cost, platforms employ keep-alive and prewarming strategies that keep function runtimes warm while allowing memory to be reclaimed during idle periods. The consequence is a ``warm container, cold data'' scenario: the function runtime and SQLite handle remain alive, but the OS page cache backing the database file has been evicted.

\subsection{Motivation}\label{sec:motivation}

The histogram-based optimization proposed by Shahrad et al. \cite{Shahrad2020} improves the accuracy of prewarming, meaning that more invocations hit warm containers. However, as this optimization succeeds, the bottleneck shifts from container startup to the storage layer: the function runtime is warm, but the OS page cache remains cold. This creates a new performance frontier where container-level optimizations have diminishing returns, and the remaining cold-start penalty resides entirely in the data path.

Du et al. \cite{Du2020} further demonstrate this trend with Catalyzer, achieving sub-millisecond function startup through checkpoint/restore and a new OS primitive called sfork. While their work validates state reuse as a powerful technique for reducing container-level cold starts, it does not address the OS page cache. The file-backed pages cached during one invocation are not preserved from checkpoint, so even with sub-millisecond container startup, the first database query after a cold cache still incurs disk I/O. The better container-level cold-start optimization becomes, the more the remaining bottleneck resides in the data path.

This is the problem we address. On our reference SQLite database, the first query under a cold OS page cache takes 523 to 1087 \textmu s, compared to 1 to 3 \textmu s when the cache is warm, a 200$\times$ to 1000$\times$ slowdown. This places the storage-layer cold-start penalty in the same latency regime as container startup itself (sub-millisecond), meaning that even with optimal container-level warming, the data path remains a first-order bottleneck.

The stakes rise further in object-storage-backed deployments. The root-to-interior-to-leaf fault chain is serial and dependent: each fault must complete before the next page address is known. On local NVMe each link costs microseconds. When SQLite is served from object storage via HTTP range requests~\cite{SqliteWebVfs, Turbolite}, each link becomes a multi-millisecond GET, amplifying the same chain by three to four orders of magnitude. Our measurements quantify this chain at the local-storage tier. The same dependent traversal structure motivates analogous cost accounting at the remote-storage tier, which we do not measure here.

\subsection{Challenges and Problem Statement}\label{sec:challenges}

Prefetching the critical B+tree pages before the first query executes seems a natural solution to the cold-data problem. Yet existing approaches to SQLite prefetching face three fundamental challenges that have not been simultaneously addressed.

\textbf{Challenge 1: Targeting.} The OS page cache operates at the granularity of file blocks, with no understanding of SQLite's internal B+tree structure. OS-level readahead detects only sequential access patterns and misses the non-sequential root-to-leaf traversal path that characterizes database queries. Application-level techniques that blindly cache entire working sets waste I/O on pages irrelevant to the critical first query. Without visibility into SQLite's page types, prefetching either misses the target entirely or overshoots with wasteful I/O.

\textbf{Challenge 2: Cost Accounting.} Even when the right pages are identified, prefetching is not free. Prior work optimizes first-query latency under the implicit assumption that improvement there translates directly to faster end-to-end performance. In the serverless critical path, however, a user request blocks on the function's response, so any time spent on prefetching directly extends the response latency. This preprocessing overhead consists of OS-syscall-level operations, including cold file open and per-page deliver, whose cost scales with the size of the prefetch set. When this overhead exceeds the first-query latency reduction, prefetching makes cold starts slower, not faster.

\textbf{Challenge 3: Deployability.} Serverless environments impose strict execution constraints. Functions run in restricted contexts where modifying the runtime, OS kernel, or database engine is often infeasible. Existing solutions that require deep forks of SQLite, custom FTL modifications, or kernel-level extensions are not deployable in production FaaS settings. A practical solution must operate entirely at the application layer, using only standard OS interfaces, without privileged access.

These three challenges are interdependent. Solving any two without the third yields a solution that is either ineffective (poor targeting), misleading (ignored overhead), or impractical (infeasible to deploy). This paper addresses all three simultaneously.

\subsection{Our Contribution}\label{sec:contribution}

This paper presents a frugal, non-intrusive SQLite prefetch framework for serverless cold starts that systematically addresses the triple challenge outlined above. Our contributions are:

\textbf{Contribution 1: Critical-path cost accounting for cold-start prefetch.} Our primary contribution is a methodology that charges prefetch preprocessing to the user-visible critical path, whereas prior work optimizes only the post-prefetch first-query latency $t_{\text{fq}}$. We decompose cold-start latency into a cold file open ($t_{\text{open}}$, approximately 230 \textmu s, per-layout constant), per-page delivery ($t_{\text{deliver}}$, whose cost scales with the size of the prefetch set), and the first query itself, and we evaluate end-to-end latency under two deployment boundaries directly aligned with serverless execution: a standalone external warmer that pays the cold open, and a warm-process/integrated model that reuses an already-open SQLite handle. We further separate page \emph{selection} from page \emph{delivery} so that selection accuracy and delivery residency are not conflated. This methodology exposes, to our knowledge for the first time, the systematic first-query versus end-to-end trade-off in SQLite cold-start prefetching.

\textbf{Contribution 2: An evidence-backed frugal-coverage design rule.} Charging preprocessing reverses the naive ranking: the strategy that best minimizes first-query latency, a full-working-set cache dump, regresses broad-workload end-to-end cold starts by about an order of magnitude because delivery cost dominates. The robust operating point is instead a small-footprint set that covers the B+tree interior navigation path, which yields roughly $25$ to $30\%$ warm-process end-to-end reduction across the evaluated pure-hit workloads with a footprint under 2 MB. We are candid about what the structural lever does and does not buy: at matched footprint, a frequency-ranked dump is statistically indistinguishable from the type-aware selector, and a corrected ablation (whose estimator we fix with a trace-order-independent tie-break, Section~\ref{sec:eval-attribution}) further shows that leaf-frequency selection alone is essentially a tie and that random leaves can slow end-to-end latency. Structural page-type knowledge therefore earns its place not as a uniquely faster selector but as an interpretable, structurally grounded way to \emph{guarantee} navigation-path coverage: the structural criterion (interior versus leaf) is workload-independent and does not spend a leaf budget while the navigation path is still cold, even though the concrete observed-interior set it selects may remain workload-specific. Adding hot leaves is an \emph{incremental} optimization that pays off only under an independently verified concentrated hotspot. We also evaluate a physical layout rewriter that clusters interior pages at the file head and find it a negative result under our workloads.

\textbf{Contribution 3: A deployment-compatible toolchain, workstation robustness validation, and FaaS first-query portability.} The framework that operationalizes the cost accounting works entirely at the application layer using only standard POSIX interfaces (\texttt{mmap}, \texttt{mincore}, \texttt{madvise}). It requires no modification to SQLite, the OS kernel, the FTL, or the serverless runtime, and no privileged access, making it an enabler for the study rather than a novel artifact in itself. We validate the design rule across six robustness axes: write churn that perturbs page layout (50k mutations across 11 checkpoints), sub-working-set RAM pressure that triggers page reclaim, multi-process cache sharing with cadence-based re-warm, workload instantiation with 10 independent random seeds and bootstrap 95\% confidence intervals, database scaling by a factor of 10 ($\sim$103 MiB to approximately 1 GiB), and static-plan staleness under a moving read hotspot. Separately, we test deployability and first-query effectiveness portability on a genuine, separate-host Apache OpenWhisk deployment. The deployment covers all 65 workstation-matched cells, comprising 55 first-query effectiveness cells and 10 libprefetch delivery-order cells. Within the 55-cell first-query domain, all 41 cells that are strongly effective on the workstation remain effective on OpenWhisk, and per-cell first-query effectiveness ranks correlate at Spearman $\rho\approx0.76$ to $0.81$. This validates deployability and first-query effectiveness portability, not the end-to-end design rule, which the workstation robustness axes establish. We state it as a bounded first-query result, not a claim of commercial-FaaS validation, hardware portability, or an isolated runtime-only effect.

\textbf{Scope of evidence.} We separate what each platform measures. On the bare-metal workstation we directly \emph{measure} the first-query, deliver, and cold-open terms and \emph{instantiate} two deployment boundaries (the warm-process/integrated cold-data model and the standalone external-warmer accounting model), which are faithful models of serverless keep-alive behavior rather than traces from a commercial platform. The workstation is the sole basis for the end-to-end cost accounting and the frugal-coverage design rule. On the separate-host Apache OpenWhisk deployment we directly \emph{measure} deployability and relative first-query effectiveness (with the libprefetch delivery-order cells where applicable), not the end-to-end boundaries. We therefore do \emph{not} establish cross-platform end-to-end ranking portability, absolute-latency portability, hardware portability, or commercial-FaaS generality. We also do \emph{not} directly measure commercial FaaS providers, distinct hardware classes, or the remote object-storage VFS tier. For these we claim only methodological relevance and structural analogy, since the same dependent root-to-leaf fault chain motivates analogous cost accounting at the remote tier, and we leave systematic validation to future work.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

\subsection{OS-Level Prefetching and Disk Scheduling}\label{sec:os-prefetch}

OS-level prefetching begins with Smith~\cite{Smith1978}, whose One Block Lookahead exploits sequential access and formalizes a cost model for when to prefetch. Like all history-based prediction, it risks polluting the cache on misprediction. Iyer and Druschel~\cite{Iyer2001} attack a scheduling-layer symptom, ``deceptive idleness,'' where a work-conserving scheduler switches away from a process during its brief compute phase, with anticipatory scheduling that waits a short interval for the next nearby request. Both trade time for locality because the OS lacks application-level semantics, and both assume seek-dominated HDDs. On NVMe, where random access costs microseconds, the seek-avoidance premise no longer holds.

Our cold-start setting has no meaningful history for the first-query path. Rather than predict, we deduce the required page set from SQLite's B+tree structure: \texttt{mincore} observes what is already resident and \texttt{madvise} prefetches what the traversal structurally requires, replacing both history and time-based speculation with deterministic structural knowledge.

\subsection{Buffer-Pool Warming and Learned Prefetching}\label{sec:buffer-warming}

Buffer management was formalized by Effelsberg and Härder~\cite{Effelsberg1984}, who defined buffer search, frame allocation, and replacement under the FIX-UNFIX interface. Learned prefetchers build on this foundation. Chen et al.~\cite{Chen2021} frame page prediction as classification, reporting 76 to 87\% precision/recall for ML models against 20\% for a LookAhead baseline, but evaluate prediction accuracy rather than end-to-end latency and quantify neither inference overhead nor the cost of mis-prefetched pages. Their traces are collected warm-start, which does not transfer to a cold cache with no historical trace.

Closest in problem framing is Pre-Buffer~\cite{Yi2026}, which targets a ``buffer cold-start problem'' (hit-rate collapse when hotspots shift) by recording per-cycle hot pages and predicting the next cycle's hot set via similarity metrics, loaded by a background thread. Pre-Buffer explicitly faults Chen et al.\ for ignoring prefetch overhead, yet itself measures only hit-rate recovery and total execution time. Its background thread keeps overhead off the user-facing path. In our serverless cold start the prefetch instead runs \emph{on} the critical path before the first query, which is exactly what makes syscall-level cost accounting a first-order requirement rather than a secondary concern, and motivates our decomposition of preprocessing into open and deliver terms.

Two adjacent results reinforce our design rule. Leaper~\cite{Yang2020}, a learned LSM prefetcher, depends on access skew, matching our finding (Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}) that hotspot-based selection helps only when a natural hot set exists. Funke et al.~\cite{Funke2022} show, in query compilation, that JIT compilation latency can dominate short-query response and quantify the break-even point where a lightweight IR (Flounder) beats heavyweight LLVM. This is the same critical-path lesson in the compute domain that we draw in the I/O domain: preprocessing cost must be charged, and a frugal mechanism can replace a heavy one.

\subsection{Application-Directed Prefetching}\label{sec:app-directed}

VanDeBogart et al.~\cite{VanDeBogart2009} propose libprefetch, which lets an application declare future read patterns so the OS can issue them in C-LOOK order. By minimizing seek distance it accelerates SQLite table scans by up to 20$\times$ on HDD-bound workloads, demonstrating the value of bridging the application--storage semantic gap.

Three differences separate it from our regime. First, its aggressive reorder buffers (hundreds of MB) pay off only when HDD seek time (5--10 ms) dominates. On NVMe the bottleneck is page-cache-miss and per-syscall cost, so frugality rather than volume is optimal when every prefetched page extends critical-path latency. Second, its SQLite integration modifies roughly 500 lines of engine source, violating the non-intrusive constraint of restricted serverless runtimes, whereas we modify nothing and use only \texttt{mmap}/\texttt{mincore}/\texttt{madvise}. Third, although its reported runtimes include prefetch time, at HDD millisecond scale the microsecond syscall overhead is negligible and need not be decomposed. In our cold-start path the first query completes in hundreds of microseconds, so that overhead is first-order and the objective shifts from maximizing I/O throughput to minimizing end-to-end critical-path latency.

\subsection{SQLite and mmap: Virtual-Memory Assisted Caching}\label{sec:mmap}

Crotty et al.~\cite{Crotty2022} critique \texttt{mmap} as a buffer-pool replacement (unpredictable writeback, signal-based error handling, no transactional control, and poor NVMe scalability), while noting one defensible case: read-only workloads whose working set fits in memory. Our cold-start prefetch sits squarely in that exception (a read-only first query, a $\sim$103 MiB reference DB, a sub-2 MB hotset), and, unlike their target, we do not use \texttt{mmap} as a buffer pool at all: SQLite's own pager retains caching and recovery, while we use \texttt{mmap}/\texttt{mincore}/\texttt{madvise(MADV\_WILLNEED)} purely as an application-layer prefetch-hint channel. Our RAM-pressure experiments confirm the hotset still delivers fully under sub-working-set caps where cache dumps collapse.

Leis et al.~\cite{Leis2023} extend the line with vmcache, retaining DBMS-controlled eviction via \texttt{madvise(MADV\_DONTNEED)}, but find Linux page-table primitives do not scale (TLB shootdowns consume up to 79\% of CPU at 1.5M ops/s) and therefore add the exmap kernel module. Their solution targets high-frequency steady state through kernel modification. We occupy the orthogonal corner, low-frequency (about 92 \texttt{madvise} calls per cold start), no-root, and application-layer, where standard interfaces suffice.

\subsection{SQLite Write-Path Optimizations}\label{sec:write-path}

A separate strand optimizes SQLite's \emph{write} path in mobile and embedded settings: Oh et al.~\cite{Oh2015} (B+tree, pager, and journaling changes plus phase-change-memory hardware), Kang et al.~\cite{Kang2013} (transaction semantics pushed into an X-FTL layer), and the mobile-storage analyses of Kim et al.~\cite{Kim2012} and Jeong et al.~\cite{Jeong2013}. All require invasive engine or firmware modification and target write amplification, making them orthogonal to our non-intrusive read cold-start focus.

This write-centric literature leaves read cold-start unexamined. Gaffney et al.~\cite{Gaffney2022}, the most comprehensive academic SQLite evaluation, prewarm by design (``before running the SSB queries, we scan each table with a \texttt{SELECT *}''), treating cold start as noise to be eliminated rather than measured. Their Lookahead Information Passing rests on the same premise as ours, that structural knowledge beats a blind mechanism, and we apply it to the cold-start phase their warm-start protocol renders invisible.

\subsection{Serverless Cold-Start}\label{sec:serverless-coldstart}

Serverless cold start is well characterized at the platform level: container reuse is industry-wide~\cite{Wang2018} and invocation intervals are too variable to predict perfectly~\cite{Shahrad2020}, establishing our ``warm container, cold data'' premise (Section~\ref{sec:warm-container}). At the compute layer, Catalyzer~\cite{Du2020} reaches sub-millisecond startup via checkpoint/restore and sfork, but does not restore the OS page cache, so the first post-cache query still faults to disk. Our storage-layer work is complementary and stays at the application layer.

At the storage layer, SQLite increasingly runs from edge and object-storage backends~\cite{CloudflareD1,SqliteWebVfs,Litestream,SqlJsHttpvfs,Turbolite}, where a custom VFS turns each page I/O into an HTTP range GET and cold start becomes the headline metric~\cite{Turbolite}. Most relevant is the \texttt{.dbi} helper of \texttt{sqlite\_web\_vfs}~\cite{SqliteWebVfs}, which gathers navigation-critical interior and metadata pages into a compact sidecar the VFS prefetches, validating interior-skeleton prefetching in production. It differs from our work on three points. First, it is a sidecar for a read-only HTTP VFS, whereas we operate on the local page cache, add no file, and support read-write workloads. Second, it publishes no cost accounting of the prefetch itself, the gap we fill. Third, its selection stops at page types, whereas we additionally evaluate an access-frequency leaf lever that Section~\ref{sec:eval-attribution} shows helps only under a verified hotspot. The tiers differ (OS page cache versus VFS-over-HTTP), but the cost-accounting methodology and interior-first selection transfer.

\section{SQLite Cold-Start Fundamentals}\label{sec:fundamentals}

\subsection{B+tree Storage and Page Types}\label{sec:btree}

SQLite stores an entire database in a single file, organized internally as fixed-size pages of 4 KB. Each logical table or index corresponds to one B+tree. Pages are classified into two roles: \textit{interior pages} store keys and child pointers, while \textit{leaf pages} contain the actual row data or index entries. The architecture is detailed in the authoritative SQLite evaluation by Gaffney et al.~\cite{Gaffney2022}. In our reference database of 600,000 rows ($\sim$103 MiB), there are 92 interior pages, which is 51 table interior and 41 index interior, totaling 368 KB and 26,239 leaf pages. A subtle but important detail is that SQLite page 1 holds the database header and the root of the \texttt{sqlite\_master} schema B+tree. The root page of each user table is stored in the \texttt{rootpage} field of \texttt{sqlite\_master} and is typically a low page number, not necessarily page 1.

The structural arithmetic that follows from standard B+tree fanout is simple but consequential: interior pages constitute only 0.35\% of the database (368 KB of $\sim$103 MiB), yet every query must traverse from root to leaf, and thus every query requires at least the interior pages along its path. For a single point query, only the pages on one root-to-leaf path (approximately the tree height, typically 3 to 4 pages) must reside in memory. Over a full workload, the accumulated set of interior pages that are visited converges to the complete set of 92. This distinction between a single query's path and the full workload's interior set is critical: it explains why a frugal prefetch can target only a few pages for a specific query, while a workload-aware strategy might need the full interior skeleton. This structural fact is not a novel discovery. It is a direct consequence of B+tree fanout arithmetic, but it provides the theoretical foundation for our targeted prefetch approach: the pages that dominate cold-start cost are few in number and lie on the root-to-leaf navigation path that every query must traverse.

\subsection{Cold-Start Mechanics}\label{sec:coldstart-mechanics}

When the OS page cache is empty, SQLite's first query must traverse the B+tree from root to leaf by fetching every missing page from disk. Each interior page that is absent from the cache triggers a random disk read. On commodity NVMe storage, each such fault typically incurs 5 to 100 \textmu s of latency. The cumulative effect of these sequential page faults, each blocking the query's progress, produces the cold-start slowdown quantified in Section~\ref{sec:motivation} (523 to 1087 \textmu s cold vs. 1 to 3 \textmu s warm, a 200$\times$ to 1000$\times$ penalty).

The root cause is structural: without prefetching, the query pays the full cost of fetching every page on the critical path, one fault at a time. The opportunity is equally structural: because the interior page set is small and known in advance, a prefetch that brings these pages into the OS page cache before the first query executes could eliminate these faults entirely. Whether such prefetching actually improves end-to-end performance, however, depends on two factors that are the subjects of the following sections: which pages to target (Section~\ref{sec:strategy}) and what preprocessing cost the prefetch itself incurs (Section~\ref{sec:methodology}).

\subsection{The Warm-Container, Cold-Data Measurement Model}\label{sec:measurement-model}

Section~\ref{sec:warm-container} established that the ``warm container, cold data'' state arises systematically in production serverless deployments: platforms keep function runtimes warm via container reuse and keep-alive policies, while the OS page cache backing the database file is evicted during idle periods. Our measurement model instantiates exactly this state. We keep the process-layer long-lived structures (the SQLite handle, prepared statements, and the \texttt{mmap} mapping) warm across measurements, while clearing every software cache below the process layer before each measurement. This models the serverless wake-up scenario where the function runtime is alive but the database pages have been reclaimed.

This design reflects three modeling choices that are deliberate rather than compromises. First, \textit{fidelity to deployment}: serverless platforms that reuse containers preserve the runtime and handle state, so the only layer that must be re-established is the OS page cache. Keeping the process warm while clearing the page cache directly mirrors this architecture. Second, \textit{variable isolation}: SQLite parser and optimizer startup time is a constant overhead independent of prefetch strategy, so including it would add noise without illuminating the prefetch mechanism. Third, \textit{reproducibility}: starting a fresh process for each measurement introduces 50 to 200 \textmu s of initialization noise, requiring more repetitions to suppress, whereas our protocol reduces this variance and yields cleaner comparisons across strategies.

\section{Cold-Start Cost Model and Measurement Methodology}\label{sec:methodology}

\subsection{The End-to-End Cost Model}\label{sec:cost-model}

The first-query latency $t_{\text{fq}}$ captures only the SQL query's wall-clock time. It does not include the preprocessing overhead of issuing prefetch hints. From the user's perspective, however, the cold-start latency that matters is the end-to-end time from function wake-up to query completion. Any time spent on prefetching directly extends the response latency. In serverless deployments, this distinction is particularly acute: a user request blocks on the function's response, so every microsecond of preprocessing contributes directly to perceived latency.

We decompose prefetch preprocessing into two OS-syscall-level terms:

\begin{itemize}
    \item $t_{\text{open}}$: the cost of a cold file open on the SQLite database, approximately 230~\textmu s, constant for a given layout and independent of the prefetch strategy.
    \item $t_{\text{deliver}}$: the per-page cost of issuing prefetch operations, whether \texttt{madvise} hints or synchronous \texttt{pread} calls. This term scales linearly with the size of the hotset.
\end{itemize}

The warmer reports both terms separately (as \texttt{open\_us} and \texttt{deliver\_us} in the harness output), with the total preprocessing time $t_{\text{prep}} = t_{\text{open}} + t_{\text{deliver}}$. The end-to-end cold-start latency is then the sum of preprocessing and the first-query latency itself. We define two deployment models that directly correspond to serverless execution patterns:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:e2e_std}
    \text{e2e}_{\text{standalone}} = t_{\text{open}} + t_{\text{deliver}} + t_{\text{fq}}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}\label{eq:e2e_warm}
    \text{e2e}_{\text{warm}} = t_{\text{deliver}} + t_{\text{fq}}
\end{equation}

where $t_{\text{fq}}$ is the first-query latency measured by the harness.

The two models differ only by the presence of $t_{\text{open}}$:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Standalone warmer (Eq.~\ref{eq:e2e_std})} corresponds to an external warmer process, for example a sidecar that performs prefetching and must cold-open the database file independently. This is the pessimistic upper bound, modeling an external prefetching agent rather than an integrated deployment.
    \item \textbf{Warm-process / integrated (Eq.~\ref{eq:e2e_warm})} corresponds to the ``warm container, cold data'' scenario instantiated in Section~\ref{sec:measurement-model}: the application is already running, the SQLite handle is already open, and prefetch reuses the existing handle without paying a second cold open. This is the model we advocate for serverless keep-alive and edge worker deployments.
\end{itemize}

Three measurements support this cost model. First, $t_{\text{fq}}$ is measured directly by the harness. Second, $t_{\text{open}}$ and $t_{\text{deliver}}$ are self-reported by the warmer, using the same engine for both \texttt{pread} and async modes, differing only in delivery mechanism. Third, the baseline first-query latency uses the handle opened before cache clearing (Section~\ref{sec:measurement-model}), so the baseline contains no \texttt{open} cost under either deployment model. The baseline is thus identical for both models: $\text{baseline} = t_{\text{fq}}$ with no preprocessing.

The hotset itself is generated offline by a separate pipeline: a cold clear, a workload warmup, and a \texttt{mincore} snapshot. This generation is a one-time or periodic cost that is amortized across many cold starts. As we show in the churn experiments of Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}, on our stationary read workloads the hotset does not decay under 50k write mutations, so recomputation is infrequent there. A read hotspot that itself moves over time is a separate axis (Section~\ref{sec:eval-robustness}), under which a static frequency plan does need refreshing. We therefore do not include this offline selection cost in the per-event critical-path end-to-end latency, which includes only delivery and the first query.

The trade-off quantified by this cost model is the foundation of our core observation in Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}: when $t_{\text{deliver}}$ is small relative to $t_{\text{fq}}$, prefetching improves end-to-end performance, and when $t_{\text{deliver}}$ dominates, even the best first-query reduction can make cold starts slower.

\subsection{Selection--Delivery Decomposition}\label{sec:selection-delivery}

The \texttt{madvise(MADV\_WILLNEED)} hint is asynchronous and does not guarantee that pages are resident by the time the first query executes. The kernel records the hint and initiates background readahead, then returns immediately. Whether a particular page is actually loaded before the first query depends on scheduling, I/O completion timing, and the kernel's readahead window. Consequently, the observed first-query latency under async hints conflates two orthogonal factors: the accuracy of the prefetch set selected by the targeting strategy (\textit{selection}) and the degree to which that set is actually resident when the query starts (\textit{delivery}). Our methodology isolates these factors by running each hotset under two delivery modes, summarized in Table~\ref{tab:delivery-modes}.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.2\linewidth}
                >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.30\linewidth}
                >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{0.35\linewidth}}
\toprule
\textbf{Mode} & \textbf{Behavior} & \textbf{Measures} \\
\midrule
\texttt{pread} (oracle) & Synchronous blocking read that returns only when all pages in the hotset are resident & $t_{\text{fq,pread}}$: the lower bound on first-query latency achievable if the selected hotset is perfectly delivered \\
Async (\texttt{madvise} / \texttt{fadvise}) & Non-blocking hint, kernel schedules I/O asynchronously & $t_{\text{fq,async}}$ and \texttt{delivery\_pct}: the practical delivery rate under real system conditions \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Two delivery modes used to isolate selection from delivery. The same hotset is used for both modes, and only the delivery mechanism differs.}
\label{tab:delivery-modes}
\end{table}

The oracle mode is not a deployable strategy. Its synchronous nature imposes millisecond-scale preprocessing that would defeat the purpose of low-latency cold starts. It serves as a lower bound on first-query latency: $t_{\text{fq,pread}}$ represents the best possible first-query latency achievable if the selected hotset is fully resident before the query starts. The async mode captures the practical case. The gap between them is the delivery loss:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:delivery_loss}
    \text{delivery\_loss} = t_{\text{fq,async}} - t_{\text{fq,pread}}
\end{equation}

Real deployments fall between these extremes. Bare async hints provide the lower bound on preprocessing effort, no synchronous I/O, but may leave pages unloaded. At the other end, synchronous delivery guarantees residency at the cost of blocking the critical path. Intermediate mechanisms, such as issuing hints earlier in the function lifecycle, using \texttt{readahead(2)} with explicit control, or employing \texttt{io\_uring} for non-blocking but guaranteed I/O, can shift the delivery rate toward the oracle bound. Our evaluation focuses on the pure async case as the most widely deployable baseline, while the oracle bound defines the performance ceiling that stronger delivery mechanisms could approach.

The kernel's readahead window caps the amount of data loaded by a single \texttt{madvise(WILLNEED)} range. With the default \texttt{read\_ahead\_kb} = 128 on our platform, a single range hint can theoretically load at most approximately $2 \times (128 / 4) = 64$ pages. In practice, on the scattered layout, we observe only 32 of the 92 requested pages delivered for a single range hint, so range-based hints on scattered layouts deliver only a fraction of the requested set. Our preferred strategies, the per-page targeted strategies that we define in Section~\ref{sec:strategy}, issue per-page hints rather than range hints, so each page is requested individually and they avoid this window limitation entirely. All measurements are conducted with \texttt{read\_ahead\_kb} fixed at the system default of 128 KB, which maximizes external validity. We record this parameter with every run and do not sweep other values in this study.

One potential concern is that our harness measures the async first-query latency immediately after the warmer returns, giving the kernel no extra time to complete background I/O. This tight timing could, in principle, overestimate the delivery loss: if a real application had additional work to perform between prefetching and the first query, readahead might finish in time. We directly tested this possibility with an intermediate-delivery sweep, inserting sleeps of 5, 20, and 50 ms between the hint dispatch and the first query on Scattered-Zipf and Tail-Mixed with representative strategies (\texttt{Skel-5}, \texttt{Skel}, and \texttt{Skel+10}, defined in Section~\ref{sec:strategy}). The results confirm two findings. First, the hotset is already 100\% resident at sleep=0: the per-page hints deliver the entire set before the harness moves to the query, so there is no outstanding I/O to wait for. Second, the residual gap $t_{\text{fq,async}} - t_{\text{fq,pread}}$ on Scattered-Zipf remains stable across all sleep durations, and the major fault counts are identical between the two modes. This gap is therefore a real and persistent cost difference, not a measurement artifact caused by insufficient post-hint delay. The exact mechanism underlying this gap remains a labeled conjecture. It may reflect the fact that synchronous \texttt{pread} warms the kernel's readahead state more effectively than one-shot async hints, but confirming this hypothesis requires root access to sweep \texttt{read\_ahead\_kb}, which our experimental environment lacks. We therefore conclude only that the gap is real and measurable, and leave its root cause to future investigation.

\subsection{Measurement Protocol}\label{sec:protocol}

All measurements are conducted on bare-metal commodity hardware (detailed in Section~\ref{sec:setup}). Every cell reports \texttt{majflt} $>0$, confirming that results reflect physical disk I/O rather than simulator timing models.

The benchmark harness executes each measurement cell in a sequence of phases that instantiates the model of Section~\ref{sec:measurement-model}:

Three protocol rules ensure measurement cleanliness and reproducibility. First, we perform double \texttt{mincore} verification: once immediately after cache clearing to confirm that the cold state is indeed empty (residual residency $<1\%$), and again after prefetching to measure the delivery rate that the first query actually sees. Second, any cell with residual cold residency exceeding 1\% is excluded from the aggregate results, ensuring that every reported measurement starts from a genuinely cold cache. Third, the post-prefetch residency check is performed inside the harness at microsecond granularity using \texttt{mincore}. We do not use external utilities for this check, as their approximately 100 ms overhead would allow asynchronous readahead to load additional pages after prefetch completes, artificially inflating the observed async delivery rate and contaminating the first-query measurement. Cold residency is 0\% across all reported cells in Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}.

For reproducibility, we record the system environment for every run, including kernel version, disk, readahead setting (\texttt{read\_ahead\_kb}, fixed at 128), governor, EPP mode, and load average. The CPU frequency is pinned via EPP performance mode. We verified boost behavior directly to ensure that the frequency ramps to maximum before each timed measurement. The hotset for each strategy is frozen via a checksum and verified by the harness before use, ensuring that selection results are deterministic and do not drift across runs.

\subsection{Statistical Methodology}\label{sec:stats}

Each (workload, layout, strategy, delivery mode) cell is repeated 10 times (n=10 reps) in rep-major order, meaning all 10 repetitions of a cell are run consecutively before moving to the next cell, which reduces cross-cell environmental drift. We report the median for each cell, as it is more robust than the mean for cold-start latency distributions, which exhibit long tails due to random I/O variation. Because the cost-model terms are additive within a single repetition, we compute each end-to-end quantity (Equations~\ref{eq:e2e_std} and~\ref{eq:e2e_warm}) \emph{per repetition}, pairing each repetition's $t_{\text{deliver}}$ and $t_{\text{fq}}$ from the same cold event, and report the median over the ten per-repetition end-to-end values. We do \emph{not} sum the per-term medians. Where decomposition tables and stacked-bar figures show the individual terms ($t_{\text{open}}$, $t_{\text{deliver}}$, $t_{\text{fq}}$), those per-term medians are given for attribution only and may differ slightly from the reported end-to-end median, because the median is not additive. We also report the 95th percentile and standard deviation where applicable. All improvement percentages are computed via paired comparison against the baseline from the same batch. This eliminates cross-batch absolute drift from the effect size calculation.

The effect sizes we observe are substantially larger than per-rep measurement noise. First-query improvements range from 30\% to 90\%, and end-to-end latency differences span an order of magnitude between strategies. We therefore do not rely on marginal statistical significance for our headline conclusions. For differences that fall within the noise floor, we do not claim rankings. For example, the first-query latency of strategy \texttt{Dump} (a cache-dump strategy defined in Section~\ref{sec:strategy}) differs by less than 3 \textmu s across the three layouts, which is below the per-measurement noise level. We therefore conclude that \texttt{Dump} is layout-agnostic rather than ranking the layouts.

To quantify whether our conclusions generalize beyond a single workload instantiation, we perform a 10-seed workload sensitivity analysis. For each of Scattered-Zipf, Uniform-100K, and Tail-Mixed, we generate 10 independent random instantiations of the query stream (same database, same number of operations) and run the full measurement matrix on each seed. For each (workload, layout, strategy), we compute the per-seed effect as the percentage improvement of the strategy over its paired baseline within the same seed. This pairing cancels cross-seed machine-state drift. We then compute a bootstrap 95\% confidence interval for the mean effect using 10,000 percentile-method resamples. We additionally require sign consistency as a cross-check: we count how many of the 10 seeds show an improvement (negative effect). With 9 or more seeds agreeing in sign, this is approximately equivalent to a sign test with $p < 0.05$.

We acknowledge a statistical limitation: with n=10 seeds, the percentile bootstrap CI may under-cover at the tails. We therefore use sign consistency as a distribution-free cross-validation. We classify an effect as \textit{robust} if its CI excludes zero, \textit{directional} if the CI crosses zero but at least 7 of 10 seeds agree in sign, and \textit{tie} otherwise (effects that fall within the noise and are not reliably reproducible). These three classifications are used throughout Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} to distinguish reliable findings from incidental ones.

As a stability anchor, strategy \texttt{Dump} loads the entire resident working set. Its first-query latency is independent of which key is accessed first. Across the 10-seed sweep, \texttt{Dump} first-query latency remains within 125.9 to 130.2 \textmu s, demonstrating that the sweep runs on a stable machine with no CPU throttling and that cross-seed variation arises from workload sampling rather than machine drift. Consequently, whenever a single-workload point estimate conflicts with the cross-seed CI, we defer to the cross-seed CI as the more reliable indicator.

\section{Prefetch Strategy Design}\label{sec:strategy}

\subsection{Two-Lever Selection: A Structural Interior Skeleton and a Conditional Leaf Bonus}\label{sec:dual-lever}

After cache clearing and before the first query executes, our framework issues \texttt{madvise(MADV\_WILLNEED)} hints for a selected set of pages. The delivery mechanism, whether asynchronous hints or synchronous \texttt{pread}, is orthogonal to the selection logic and was isolated in Section~\ref{sec:selection-delivery}. What distinguishes our strategies is solely which pages are selected for prefetching.

We organize the strategy space around two independent selection levers. The first lever, \textit{page-type awareness}, exploits the structural invariant of SQLite's B+tree: interior pages are few in number, lie on the root-to-leaf navigation path that any query must traverse, and together constitute only 368 KB (0.35\%) of the database. Rather than predicting future accesses from past behavior, we deduce that any query must traverse the interior navigation path, so covering the interior skeleton eliminates the dominant source of cold-start page faults. The second lever, \textit{access-frequency awareness}, observes which pages are actually resident after a workload warmup via \texttt{mincore} and prioritizes the most frequently accessed leaves. These two levers are complementary: page-type awareness provides coverage for uniform workloads with no natural hot set, while access-frequency awareness unlocks additional gains on skewed workloads where a small number of leaves dominate accesses.

Table~\ref{tab:strategies} defines the strategies we evaluate, grouped by their primary selection lever. The hotset sizes are measured on our reference $\sim$103 MiB database.

The page-type lever encompasses the \texttt{Skel-N} family, the range and per-page variants (\texttt{Skel-all-range} and \texttt{Skel-all-page}), and the interior component of \texttt{Skel}. A critical clarification is that ``first $N$ interiors by file offset'' equates to ``the top $N$ levels of the B+tree'' only when the database layout is type-aware, that is, when all interior pages are physically clustered at the file head. Under the default SQLite layout (\texttt{Default}), where interiors and leaves are interleaved, \texttt{Skel-N} simply prefetches the earliest $N$ interior pages in the file, which may bear no relation to tree depth. This distinction explains the workload-dependent behavior we quantify in Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}. The \texttt{Skel} strategy improves on \texttt{Skel-N} by prefetching only the interior pages that are actually resident after a workload warmup, determined via \texttt{mincore}. For Tail-Mixed, for example, \texttt{Skel} prefetches only 4 interior pages rather than all 92, as the query stream touches a narrow right-edge navigation path of the B+tree. Note that \texttt{Skel} uses residency observation to \emph{filter} the interior set to those actually used. It does not rank pages by frequency. The frequency lever enters only through the leaf component of \texttt{Skel+K}.

The access-frequency lever extends \texttt{Skel} by adding the most frequently accessed leaves. For a given workload, we run a warmup pass, record per-page access counts, and select the top $K$ leaves. The \texttt{Skel+K} strategies thus combine structural coverage, the interior skeleton, with workload-specific hot data, the high-frequency leaves. We sweep $K \in \{10, 50, 100, 500\}$ in Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}. The sweep itself illustrates the cost-accounting trade-off, as larger $K$ improves first-query latency but inflates delivery cost.

The \texttt{Dump} strategy serves as a contrasting extreme. It captures the full resident working set after a workload warmup, mimicking the application-side analogue of InnoDB's buffer pool dump and load. This strategy prefetches the entire working set, approximately 4,400 pages (17.7 MB) for Scattered-Zipf and Uniform-100K, and represents the ``prefetch everything'' baseline against which our frugal targeted strategies are compared. Its behavior in end-to-end latency, detailed in Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}, reveals why larger hotsets do not necessarily yield faster cold starts.

All access-pattern-based hotsets (\texttt{Skel}, \texttt{Skel+K}, and \texttt{Dump}) are generated offline by a separate pipeline: a cold cache clear, a workload warmup, and a \texttt{mincore} snapshot. As noted in Section~\ref{sec:cost-model}, this is a one-time or periodic cost that is amortized across repeated cold starts and does not appear in the per-event critical-path end-to-end latency. The hotset checksum is frozen and verified by the harness before each measurement to ensure reproducibility.

\subsection{Physical Layout Rewriting}\label{sec:layout}

The physical placement of pages within the SQLite file determines whether sequential prefetch hints can efficiently cover multiple interior pages in a single I/O operation. We evaluate three layout variants that share the same logical database content but differ in page arrangement.

The default layout, \texttt{Default}, is produced when the database is populated through standard SQLite inserts. Interior and leaf pages are interleaved throughout the file, with a scatter score of 0.96. We define the scatter score as a normalized measure of interior-page dispersion across the file, where 0 indicates perfect clustering and higher values indicate greater fragmentation. The \texttt{Vacuum} layout is generated by invoking SQLite's built-in \texttt{VACUUM} command, which rewrites the database file in insertion order but does not consider page types. In our experiments, \texttt{VACUUM} increases the scatter score to 1.13, making the layout more fragmented rather than less, and reduces the file size by approximately 3\%. This outcome establishes that \texttt{VACUUM} is not a solution to the interior-page scattering problem.

The \texttt{Clustered} layout is produced by our custom binary-level layout rewriter. The rewriter relocates all 92 interior pages to the beginning of the file, placing them contiguously in pages 2 through 93, followed by leaf pages, with freelist and overflow pages appended at the end. The rewriter patches every cross-page pointer affected by this relocation: interior page child pointers, overflow page next-page pointers, freelist next-trunk pointers, and the freelist pointer in the page 1 header. It also emits SQL to update the \texttt{rootpage} fields in \texttt{sqlite\_master}. The resulting layout achieves a scatter score of 0.0001, clustering all interior pages within the first 400 KB of the file. The rewriter passes \texttt{PRAGMA integrity\_check} on the rewritten database, confirming that all pointer references remain valid.

Figure~\ref{fig:layout-distribution} visualizes the physical distribution of the 92 interior pages under the three layouts. The contrast between the scattered default layout and the tightly clustered type-aware layout motivates the design of \texttt{Clustered}: with interior pages confined to the first 400 KB, a single range-based prefetch hint can cover the entire critical set, potentially amortizing the cold-start I/O cost into a single sequential read. We evaluate this potential in Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} and, as we preview below, find that it does not translate into end-to-end wins under our workloads.

VACUUM (\texttt{Vacuum}) does not improve clustering. The type-aware rewriter (\texttt{Clustered}) clusters all interiors within the first 400 KB of the file.}

It is important to be precise about the role of \texttt{Clustered} in our framework. Clustering interior pages at the file head is a double-edged sword. The benefit is that it enables structural prefetch strategies, such as \texttt{Skel-N}, to cover the critical interior set with a single range hint. The cost is that relocating interiors to the file head pushes leaf pages to higher offsets, which raises the no-prefetch baseline for workloads that are dominated by cold leaf faults. (A production system, \texttt{sqlite\_web\_vfs}'s \texttt{.dbi} side file~\cite{SqliteWebVfs}, sidesteps this displacement by copying navigation-critical pages rather than reordering them in place, and we return to this in Section~\ref{sec:eval-attribution}.) In our Scattered-Zipf/Uniform-100K/Tail-Mixed workload matrix, the best end-to-end performance consistently occurs with the default layout (\texttt{Default}) combined with access-pattern prefetching (\texttt{Skel} or \texttt{Skel+K}). \texttt{Clustered} does not outperform \texttt{Default} in any measured cell. The type-aware layout is therefore a negative result under our workloads, useful only in the narrow scenario where prefetching will definitely be deployed, structural targeting is the only available mechanism (for example, when no access-pattern observation is possible), and the workload is sufficiently skewed that the interior prefetch gain outweighs the leaf-offset penalty. We provide the full per-cell comparison and the precise net-win conditions in Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}. This honest assessment aligns with the framing in Section~\ref{sec:contribution}, where we introduced the layout rewriter as a design alternative we evaluated and found to be negative, rather than as a recommended deployment default.

\subsection{Deployment Integration}\label{sec:deployment}

The cost model in Section~\ref{sec:cost-model} distinguished two deployment scenarios at the level of end-to-end latency accounting. Here we describe their implementation. In the external-warmer deployment, prefetching runs as a separate process, typically a sidecar, that must cold-open the database file independently. This corresponds to Eq.~\ref{eq:e2e_std}, where the cold open cost $t_{\text{open}}$ is paid on every invocation. In the integrated warm-process deployment, the application itself issues prefetch hints after its SQLite handle is already open and before serving the first request. This corresponds to Eq.~\ref{eq:e2e_warm}, where the handle is reused and $t_{\text{open}}$ is not paid. The integrated deployment is the model we advocate for serverless keep-alive and edge worker scenarios, where the function runtime remains resident across invocations.

For multi-process deployments, such as multiple function instances on the same node sharing a database file, our framework leverages Linux's \texttt{mmap(MAP\_SHARED)} to share a single OS page cache across all processes. With SQLite's \texttt{PRAGMA mmap\_size} set to the file size, the database file is mapped into memory with \texttt{MAP\_SHARED}, and pages brought into the cache by one process become visible to all others that share the same mapping. A single prefetch operation thus benefits every process accessing the database. A private-cache configuration, where each process maintains its own buffer via standard \texttt{read()} calls, serves as a baseline for comparison. The shared-memory advantage, along with the trade-off between prefetch cadence and cache freshness, is evaluated in the cadence experiments of Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}.

\section{Experimental Setup}\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Platform}\label{sec:platform}

All experiments run on a single bare-metal commodity machine: an AMD Ryzen 9950X CPU with 64 GB of physical memory (62 GiB available) and a KINGSTON SKC3000D2048G (2 TB, NVMe Gen4 $\times$4) SSD. The operating system is Linux kernel 6.17. The kernel readahead window, \texttt{read\_ahead\_kb}, is fixed at its system default of 128 KB. We do not sweep this parameter (see Section~\ref{sec:selection-delivery}). Frequency pinning follows the protocol of Section~\ref{sec:protocol}. All experiments are conducted on this single machine and kernel. We discuss the scope and limitations of this platform in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

\subsection{Reference Database}\label{sec:refdb}

All experiments use a fixed reference database with a single table, \texttt{items(id INTEGER PRIMARY KEY, k1 INTEGER, k2 INTEGER, payload BLOB(100))}, and a secondary index on \texttt{(k1, k2)}. The database contains 600,000 rows, occupies 102.9 MiB (107{,}851{,}776 bytes), and uses 4 KB pages. The page composition, including the 92 interior pages and 26,239 leaf pages, is detailed in Section~\ref{sec:btree}. Three layout variants share the same logical content but differ in physical page arrangement: the default layout (\texttt{Default}) and the type-aware layout (\texttt{Clustered}) each contain 26,331 pages, and the \texttt{VACUUM} layout (\texttt{Vacuum}) contains 25,613 pages. A 10$\times$ scaled variant with 6,000,000 rows and 263,991 pages ($\sim$1 GiB) is used in the size-scaling experiments (Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}).

\subsection{Workloads}\label{sec:workloads}

We evaluate three measured read workloads that cover orthogonal dimensions of access-pattern variation. Scattered-Zipf is read-only point queries from a scrambled Zipfian distribution over a 100K-key active domain. Uniform-100K is uniform read-only point queries over the same 100K-key active domain, used as a matched no-skew control. Tail-Mixed is a 20K-key interval crossing the database maximum, producing approximately 50\% successful and 50\% not-found queries. Alongside these we run one database-evolution schedule, Mixed-Mutation Churn, used only to age the database between measured probes and not latency-measured. We classify the workloads into three roles. Scattered-Zipf, Uniform-100K, Tail-Mixed, and the Tail-Hit control are controlled read workloads. Latest-Aging and Short-Scan Aging are deterministic Python reconstructions of YCSB-D and YCSB-E semantics. Mixed-Mutation Churn is a mutation schedule, not a measured workload. We refer to every workload by its descriptive name throughout, which also avoids confusion with the standard YCSB core workloads A--F.

Scattered-Zipf, Uniform-100K, and Tail-Mixed are designed to represent three distinct access characteristics: skew (Scattered-Zipf), uniformity (Uniform-100K), and mixed tail-boundary lookups (Tail-Mixed). Scattered-Zipf follows the access-distribution profile of YCSB-C~\cite{Cooper2010} (read-only Zipfian, $\alpha = 0.99$), with a key permutation that scatters hot keys across the 100{,}000-key active domain, analogous to YCSB's scrambled Zipfian. Our generator reproduces the distribution rather than YCSB's exact sampling implementation. Both Scattered-Zipf and Uniform-100K follow the YCSB op-string format (\texttt{read <key>}, \texttt{update <key>}, \texttt{scan <key> <len>})\footnote{\url{https://github.com/ls4154/YCSB-cpp}}. Tail-Mixed and Mixed-Mutation Churn are custom designs specific to this study. They have no YCSB equivalent. Scattered-Zipf and Uniform-100K draw keys from a 100,000-key active domain (ids 1 through 100,000), which lies entirely within the database's 600,000-key extent. Tail-Mixed instead draws uniformly over a 20,000-key interval (ids 590,000 through 609,999) that crosses the database maximum id of 600,000, so roughly half of its lookups fall beyond the maximum and become not-found probes. Each read workload executes 100,000 operations.

Three auxiliary robustness workloads support specific controls, all on the original layout (\texttt{Default}) only. Tail-Hit is a matched pure-hit control that draws the same-size tail range entirely within the existing key space (zero not-found), and it isolates the not-found effect in the attribution analysis of Section~\ref{sec:eval-attribution}. Latest-Aging (read-latest) and Short-Scan Aging (short-ranges) are deterministic Python reconstructions of the YCSB-D and YCSB-E core semantics rather than official YCSB traces, and they drive the static-plan staleness experiment of Section~\ref{sec:eval-robustness}: Latest-Aging's read hotspot moves over time (non-stationary), whereas Short-Scan Aging's is stationary. Neither Tail-Hit nor Latest-Aging/Short-Scan Aging alters the Scattered-Zipf/Uniform-100K/Tail-Mixed first-query baselines. Hashed-Hotspot is a native YCSB hotspot read workload (hot-data-fraction $1\%$) whose hot keys are hashed across the physical key space, so the hot leaves are scattered rather than contiguous. It is used only in the cross-platform portability analysis of Section~\ref{sec:eval-portability} and is not equated with Tail-Mixed.

The three read workloads each produce the cold first-query baselines reported throughout this paper: under the default layout (\texttt{Default}), the median cold first-query latencies are 523~\textmu s for Scattered-Zipf, 749~\textmu s for Uniform-100K, and 1087~\textmu s for Tail-Mixed. These baseline values are used as the denominator for all improvement calculations in Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}. The original workload traces are published with our artifact (checksummed). The accompanying generator reproduces their distributions and drives the 10-seed sensitivity analysis of Section~\ref{sec:stats}.

\subsection{OpenWhisk Portability Deployment}\label{sec:setup-openwhisk}

The cross-platform portability analysis (Section~\ref{sec:eval-portability})
runs the same prefetch toolchain inside a genuine container-based FaaS runtime,
Apache OpenWhisk, on a separate commodity x86 host with its own consumer-grade
NVMe SSD and a different Linux kernel. We compare only relative first-query
reductions across platforms and never absolute microseconds, so the deployment
exercises the serverless runtime together with its host rather than isolating the
container-runtime dimension from the underlying hardware. The action
loads an identical bound reference database, the original-layout (\texttt{Default})
\texttt{test.db} of 26{,}331 pages, and issues prefetch hints through the same
non-privileged application-layer interfaces (\texttt{mmap}, \texttt{madvise})
used on the workstation (Contribution~3). Nothing in the container requires
kernel, FTL, or runtime modification.

Each measured invocation is one arm of an A/B pair: a same-condition baseline
arm and a strategy arm, scheduled under a block-union paired design with
position balancing. We report the \emph{fresh-handle} paired first-query, a per-invocation
handle-mode measurement, distinct from the external-warmer deployment boundary
of the cost model (Section~\ref{sec:methodology}), which is a
deployment-feasibility and relative-effectiveness signal rather than an absolute
strategy-performance estimate. Absolute microseconds are never compared between
OpenWhisk and the workstation. The comparison quantity is the relative
first-query reduction $R$ defined in Section~\ref{sec:eval-portability}.

Seven frozen campaigns comprise 5{,}756 invocations and 2{,}878 A/B pairs. The
campaign totals are bookkeeping only, and effect estimates are never pooled
across campaigns. Together the campaigns give complete coverage of the
strategy$\times$workload cells the workstation ran, 65 of 65: 55 first-query
effectiveness cells plus 10 delivery-order \texttt{LP} cells.

\section{Evaluation}\label{sec:evaluation}

Our evaluation answers three questions. \textbf{RQ1:} does a lower first-query latency imply a faster cold start? Sections~\ref{sec:eval-fq} and~\ref{sec:eval-tradeoff} show that it does not: once delivery cost is charged, the first-query ranking can reverse. \textbf{RQ2:} given critical-path cost accounting, what should we prefetch? Section~\ref{sec:eval-attribution} shows the answer is a small coverage set: interior-navigation coverage is a robust, interpretable default, hot leaves help only under an independently verified hotspot, and at matched footprint a sophisticated selector shows no demonstrated advantage over a frequency-ranked dump. \textbf{RQ3:} are the end-to-end design conclusions robust to workload and system variation, and does the underlying first-query effectiveness survive deployment in a FaaS runtime? Section~\ref{sec:eval-robustness} tests the end-to-end conclusion under workload and system variation on the controlled workstation, across six robustness axes. Section~\ref{sec:eval-portability} asks the narrower deployment question of whether the first-query effectiveness signal survives on Apache OpenWhisk, where all 41 strong-workstation first-query cells remain effective and first-query effectiveness ranks correlate at Spearman $\rho\approx0.76$ to $0.81$.

\subsection{First-Query Gains and Workload-Dependent Ceilings}\label{sec:eval-fq}

We begin by establishing the first-query latency improvements achieved by each strategy, before accounting for prefetch preprocessing in Section~\ref{sec:eval-tradeoff}. Under a cold OS page cache, the baseline first-query latencies are 523 \textmu s for Scattered-Zipf, 749 \textmu s for Uniform-100K, and 1087 \textmu s for Tail-Mixed (Section~\ref{sec:workloads}). The cache-dump strategy \texttt{Dump} achieves the lowest first-query latency across all three workloads: 108 \textmu s for Scattered-Zipf ($-79\%$), 107 \textmu s for Uniform-100K ($-86\%$), and 102 \textmu s for Tail-Mixed ($-91\%$). If the evaluation stopped at first-query latency, \texttt{Dump} would be the unambiguous winner. The targeted strategies achieve reductions ranging from $-27\%$ to $-83\%$, with the specific ceiling depending on the workload.

Table~\ref{tab:ceiling} summarizes the workload-dependent benefit ceiling for first-query improvement. Scattered-Zipf, which exhibits a skewed Zipfian access pattern, concentrates first-query probability on a small set of leaves (all pages are nonetheless cold at measurement time). Prefetching interior pages alone captures the dominant benefit, yielding $-27\%$ to $-30\%$ improvement. Adding more hot leaves through \texttt{Skel+500} reaches $-64\%$, and the full cache dump reaches $-79\%$, but the marginal gain of adding leaves beyond the interior skeleton is modest. Uniform-100K, which accesses keys uniformly at random, has no natural hot set. Every query touches a cold leaf, and interior-only prefetching is capped at approximately $-44\%$. Only the full cache dump, which loads the entire resident working set, reduces first-query latency further to $-86\%$. Tail-Mixed, which reads a high-key tail range roughly half of which lies beyond the database's maximum key, faces cold leaves on every query. The access-pattern strategy \texttt{Skel+10} breaks through the interior-only ceiling of $-39\%$ largely because the $\sim$50\% not-found probes concentrate on the rightmost leaf, which \texttt{Skel+10} prefetches, achieving $-83\%$ first-query reduction (a key-range artifact, not general tail prediction, Section~\ref{sec:eval-attribution}).

The effectiveness of structural prefetching, \texttt{Skel-N}, depends on both the workload and the physical layout. On Scattered-Zipf, prefetching the first five interior pages reaches a plateau at approximately $-27\%$ to $-30\%$ first-query reduction, and additional interior pages beyond $N=5$ provide no further benefit. Uniform-100K exhibits the same pattern: $N=5$ is already sufficient, and the plateau sits at approximately $-44\%$. Tail-Mixed, in contrast, requires nearly all 92 interior pages ($N \approx 92$): \texttt{Skel-N} selects interiors by file offset, while the interiors covering Tail-Mixed's high-key tail range sit mid-file, so offset-order selection misses them until $N$ spans nearly the full set. Prefetching only a single interior page ($N=1$) is counter-productive on Scattered-Zipf: the \texttt{madvise} dispatch overhead exceeds the coverage benefit, making first-query latency approximately $+31\%$ worse than the same-batch baseline. On Uniform-100K and Tail-Mixed, $N=1$ is slightly better than the baseline by a few percent. The mechanism behind the \texttt{Skel-N} plateau is subtle. At first-query time, both interior and leaf pages are equally cold. Prefetching interior pages removes interior faults but leaves the first leaf fault intact. After the query stream warms up, the hot leaves corresponding to frequently accessed keys become naturally resident in the cache. The interior pages, however, remain the only shared bottleneck across all queries. Prefetching the interior skeleton is therefore sufficient for workloads with stable hot keys, while workloads whose first queries strike cold leaves require more: Uniform-100K, with no natural hot set, is served only by the full dump, whereas Tail-Mixed's concentrated tail region makes access-frequency targeting effective. The effect of the hot key location, such as low-id hotspots, is secondary. The shape of the N-sweep is consistent across hotspot variants in our artifact.

The end-to-end cold-start latency that a user experiences includes both the first query and the prefetch itself. Table~\ref{tab:overhead} decomposes this preprocessing cost for each strategy.

The deliver cost $t_{\text{deliver}}$ scales linearly with the number of pages in the hotset. This deliver cost is the source of the end-to-end trade-off that we now quantify.

The cold open cost is a common-mode constant that shifts both deployment models' zero points. Algebraically, $\text{e2e}_{\text{std}} - (\text{baseline}+t_{\text{open}}) = \text{e2e}_{\text{warm}} - \text{baseline}$, so $t_{\text{open}}$ never determines the within-model verdict.

Figure~\ref{fig:e2e-stacked} makes this trade-off explicit. Where Figure~\ref{fig:firstq-bars} ranked strategies by first-query latency alone, Figure~\ref{fig:e2e-stacked} charges delivery onto the warm-process critical path, so each bar height is $\text{e2e}_{\text{warm}}$ (first query stacked with deliver) and the ranking can reverse. A large hotset such as \texttt{Dump} therefore rises far above the dashed baseline on Scattered-Zipf and Uniform-100K, becoming an end-to-end regression despite its first-query win, while the interior skeleton \texttt{Skel} sits below the baseline on all three workloads. The \texttt{Skel-5} wins there are single-instantiation only and not cross-seed robust (Table~\ref{tab:e2e-ac}).

The end-to-end accounting supports three conclusions. First, the cache-dump approach \texttt{Dump} shows that minimizing first-query latency does not guarantee end-to-end acceleration, because its deliver overhead of 0.8 to 7 ms dominates the critical path. Second, the deployment model determines the end-to-end verdict. In the external-warmer model, the cold open cost eliminates the benefit on fast workloads. In the warm-process model, which we advocate for serverless keep-alive and edge worker deployments, the targeted strategies improve end-to-end latency across all three workloads. Third, the open cost is not a prefetch tax but a deployment constant. It shifts the zero point between the two models but never determines the relative performance of prefetch strategies within either model.

\subsection{Attribution: Ablation and Competitive Baselines}\label{sec:eval-attribution}

The large single-instantiation result of Section~\ref{sec:eval-tradeoff} on Tail-Mixed combines two selection levers: page-type awareness (interior pages) and access-frequency awareness (hot leaves). We isolate their individual contributions using an exact decomposition of the \texttt{Skel+10} hotset. This attribution required correcting a subtle estimator artifact. Our original hot-leaf ranking broke frequency ties by first-seen order. On a tied-count workload this preferentially selected the earliest-observed leaves, which coincide with the measured first query and leak into the hotset. We replaced it with a deterministic, trace-order-independent tie-break (frequency descending, then page number ascending) and re-measured the affected cells in a same-batch rerun. The corrected numbers below reverse the pre-fix attribution:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{Skel}: page-type lever only, prefetching resident interiors after workload warmup.
    \item \texttt{leaf\_freq\_K10}: access-frequency lever only, prefetching the top-10 most frequently accessed leaves.
    \item \texttt{leaf\_rand\_K10}: control group, prefetching 10 leaves of the same type (leaf\_table) drawn uniformly at random, controlling for page count and type while isolating the effect of frequency ranking.
    \item \texttt{Skel+10}: union of \texttt{Skel} and \texttt{leaf\_freq\_K10}, the exact decomposition.
\end{itemize}

The control group is the crucial design element: \texttt{leaf\_rand\_K10} and \texttt{leaf\_freq\_K10} have the same page count (10) and the same page type (leaf), differing only in whether selection is frequency-based or random. Any difference between them is attributable purely to access-frequency awareness.

The corrected ablation and competitive-baseline experiments run as a single same-batch 10-seed rerun with paired baselines. Cross-seed means therefore differ from the single-instantiation numbers of Sections~\ref{sec:eval-fq} and~\ref{sec:eval-tradeoff}. Table~\ref{tab:ablation} presents the cross-seed means for Tail-Mixed. The interior-only lever, \texttt{Skel}, is $-43\%$ first-query and $-36\%$ end-to-end, and is the only \emph{robust} contributor (CI excludes zero). The random-leaf control is net-harmful in end-to-end ($+7\%$), showing that loading extra leaves without a real hotspot costs deliver time for no coverage. Crucially, the frequency-selected leaves alone, \texttt{leaf\_freq\_K10}, are only $-3\%$ end-to-end with a confidence interval that crosses zero, i.e.\ a tie: their pre-fix value of $-40\%$ was almost entirely first-op leakage. The combined strategy, \texttt{Skel+10}, reaches $-63\%$ first-query and $-55\%$ end-to-end, but is bimodal across first-op hit/miss seeds (discussed below). The attribution therefore reverses relative to the pre-fix analysis: page-type (interior) awareness is the robust lever and the enabler of any leaf benefit (a leaf is useless while its interior path is uncached), whereas leaf-frequency alone is not a robust standalone signal on this workload.

Changing the physical layout from the default (\texttt{Default}) to the type-aware layout (\texttt{Clustered}) does not change the selection story: it increases the number of interior pages prefetched by \texttt{Skel} on Tail-Mixed from 4 to 48, but the warm e2e improvement is slightly worse ($-63\%$ vs $-75\%$ for \texttt{Default}), consistent with the layout negative result we detail below.

The corrected ablation establishes an asymmetric hierarchy, not two equal levers. Page-type awareness is the robust default: it carries Uniform-100K, supplies the interior skeleton for Scattered-Zipf and Tail-Mixed, and is the prerequisite for any leaf benefit. Access-frequency leaf selection is a conditional add-on: it contributes a genuine, robust bonus only where a real hotspot exists (the Zipfian skew of Scattered-Zipf), and is a tie on the uniform-hit portion of Tail-Mixed. What looked, before the tie-break fix, like a large access-frequency contribution on Tail-Mixed was largely first-op leakage. The residual leaf signal on Tail-Mixed stems from its not-found rightmost-leaf concentration, discussed next, not from general leaf predictability.

The reason Tail-Mixed produces a large but narrow result is a property of its key range rather than of tail reads in general. Tail-Mixed draws uniformly over a high-key range whose upper half exceeds the reference database's maximum key, so approximately half of its lookups are not-found probes. Every such probe descends the B+tree right edge to the same rightmost leaf, which therefore becomes an extreme, key-range-induced hotspot. Consequently Tail-Mixed's corrected \texttt{Skel+10} result is bimodal across seeds: when the measured first query is itself a not-found probe it hits this hotspot ($\approx -70\%$), and when it is a genuine hit it falls back to the interior-skeleton regime ($\approx -31\%$), for a cross-seed mean of $-55\%$. To confirm that this is a not-found artifact rather than a general uniform-tail-read benefit, we run a matched pure-hit control on the original layout, Tail-Hit, which draws the same-size tail range entirely within the existing key space (zero not-found). On this control the corrected \texttt{Skel+10} returns to $\approx -27\%$, statistically matching interior-only \texttt{Skel} ($-28.5\%$) and a footprint-matched frequency dump ($-30.6\%$): once the not-found concentration is removed, leaf-frequency selection adds essentially nothing beyond the interior skeleton. We report the large Tail-Mixed number therefore as a scoped, mechanism-specific result, not as the paper's general finding.

We next ask whether the cache-dump strategies used in Section~\ref{sec:eval-tradeoff}, specifically \texttt{Dump}, are an unfairly weak baseline. To address this concern, we design two competitive baselines that port the page-selection core of representative prior systems onto our common substrate, SQLite over the OS page cache, so that they can be evaluated on the cold-start critical path under the same cost accounting. \texttt{Dump} ports the full-working-set dump-and-reload core of InnoDB's buffer pool dump/load: it captures the entire resident working set (approximately 4,400 pages, 17.7 MB on Scattered-Zipf/Uniform-100K, 1.8 MB on Tail-Mixed) and reloads it at cold start. \texttt{Dump-N} implements a frequency-ranked dump in the spirit of Pre-Buffer's hot-page selection~\cite{Yi2026}: our variant replays each query's root-to-leaf traversal, counts page visits, and dumps the top-$N$ ranked pages ($N \in \{14, 28, 100, 500\}$). InnoDB's partial dump ranks by LRU recency, whereas Pre-Buffer ranks by access frequency, and our arm implements the frequency variant. We deliberately reproduce only the selection mechanism, not the surrounding orchestration (InnoDB's startup-time reload and Pre-Buffer's background prefetch thread, Jaccard-similarity workload-change detection, dedicated prefetch buffer, and page-cache-bypassing Direct I/O), because that orchestration runs off the critical path and is orthogonal to our question. Neither prior system accounts for prefetch delivery cost on the user-facing critical path. By porting their selection cores into our cost-accounted first-query harness, we show that frequency-ranked selection at matched footprint ties our targeted strategy on both broad and narrow workloads, while the full-working-set dump, the InnoDB core, regresses warm-process end-to-end latency by up to $+762\%$ (cross-seed mean, as we show below). We sweep $N \in \{14, 28, 100, 500, \text{full}\}$.

Table~\ref{tab:competitive} shows the e2e warm results for a representative subset: the targeted strategy \texttt{Skel+10}, the tuned dump at its sweet spot (\texttt{Dump-14}), a larger tuned dump (\texttt{Dump-500}), and the full dump (\texttt{Dump}). The competitive sweep supports three conclusions. First, end-to-end performance monotonically worsens with dump footprint: full dump regresses by $+762\%$ (cross-seed mean) on Scattered-Zipf, compared to the single-instantiation figure of $+1300\%$ in Table~\ref{tab:e2e-ac}, and by $+730\%$ on Uniform-100K, demonstrating that the deliver cost of large hotsets is the fundamental problem, not the dump mechanism itself. Only small, frequency-ranked partial dumps win, with sweet spots at $N \approx 14$ to 28. Second, on the broad workloads Scattered-Zipf and Uniform-100K, \texttt{Dump-N} with matched footprint effectively matches \texttt{Skel+10}: Scattered-Zipf $-37\%$ (\texttt{Dump-28}) vs $-38\%$ (\texttt{Skel+10}), Uniform-100K $-27\%$ (\texttt{Dump-14}) vs $-24\%$ (\texttt{Skel+10}), and CIs overlap. Page-type knowledge is not necessary for these workloads, and frequency ranking alone suffices. Third, on the narrow workload Tail-Mixed, the corrected same-batch comparison shows \emph{no} advantage for explicit type awareness: \texttt{Skel+10} achieves $-55\%$ (CI $[-67, -42]$) and matched \texttt{Dump-14} achieves $-55\%$ (CI $[-67, -43]$), with essentially fully overlapping CIs, so the two are statistically indistinguishable. The pre-fix appearance of a robust $-72\%$ vs $-57\%$ gap was an artifact of the first-op tie-break leakage. This is consistent with the ablation: because Tail-Mixed's leaf signal comes from a not-found rightmost-leaf concentration that a frequency-ranked dump also captures, footprint-matched frequency ranking recovers a similar page set. Explicit page typing is an interpretable way to \emph{guarantee} interior-skeleton coverage, but it does not uniquely win on Tail-Mixed.

Across all three workloads, \texttt{Skel+10} ties, but does not beat, a footprint-matched frequency-ranked dump. What matters is a small footprint combined with sound page selection. Page-type awareness earns its place as an \emph{interpretable} mechanism that \emph{guarantees} interior-skeleton coverage independent of the observed access sample, rather than as a technique that uniquely outperforms frequency ranking.

Two prior-art arms cited in Section~\ref{sec:related} remain, each isolating a dimension the competitive baselines above hold fixed.

\textbf{Delivery order (libprefetch).} The competitive baselines above vary the prefetch \emph{set} at a fixed delivery order. The core contribution of libprefetch~\cite{VanDeBogart2009} is the opposite, varying delivery \emph{order} (C-LOOK offset sorting) at a fixed set. We reproduce it as an ordering-only comparison: two arms deliver the identical $\sim$18\,MB working set (whose sorted-order page set is identical to \texttt{Dump}), one in ascending file-offset order (\texttt{LP-sorted}), one shuffled (\texttt{LP-shuf}). On NVMe the ordering penalty survives only in the deliver term: synchronous \texttt{pread} delivery is $10$ to $16\times$ slower shuffled than sorted ($15.6\times$ Scattered-Zipf, $15.1\times$ Uniform-100K, $10.5\times$ Tail-Mixed), hundreds of milliseconds of avoidable serialized transfer. First-query latency is essentially unchanged between the two orderings (a warm control: both arms deliver the same pages). The entire ordering effect falls in the deliver term, orders of magnitude larger than any first-query difference. The penalty is specific to synchronous \texttt{pread}, which serializes the transfer. Asynchronous \texttt{fadvise} dispatch, which only hands the kernel a hint, shows no ordering effect (deliver ratio $\approx 1.0$ on all three workloads). Without a block-level trace we make no device-mechanism claim beyond this synchronous/asynchronous split. libprefetch is thus orthogonal to our set-selection contribution: a delivery-scheduling optimization whose only NVMe residue is a deliver-term cost that leaves first-query latency untouched.

\textbf{Learned page-transition prefetching (Chen lineage).} The second arm is a learned prefetcher in the lineage of Chen et al.~\cite{Chen2021}: a first-order Markov page-transition model that computes finite-horizon expected page visits from the B$^+$tree root and prefetches the top-$N$. We evaluate it under leave-one-seed-out (LOSO) cross-validation across the 10 workload seeds (train on nine, test on the held-out seed) so no test trace informs its own prefetch set. At matched footprint it reduces cold first-query latency by $-39.6\%$ (Scattered-Zipf), $-36.6\%$ (Uniform-100K), and $-65.4\%$ (Tail-Mixed), a $-37\%$ to $-69\%$ range across cells with every 95\% CI excluding zero, at $100\%$ delivery on verified-cold caches. It ties footprint-matched frequency ranking and targeted prefetch on Uniform-100K and Tail-Mixed (overlapping CIs). On Scattered-Zipf, the learned model reaches about $-40\%$, compared with roughly $-51\%$ for the same-budget frequency-ranked and hot-leaf arms, whose confidence intervals overlap, so we do not claim a statistically resolved ranking. The learned transition model provides no demonstrated advantage over the simpler frequency-ranked baseline. (An earlier single-fold analysis reported a more optimistic held-out figure, but the honest LOSO mean supersedes it.) These two arms therefore reinforce the central finding: neither delivery-order scheduling nor a learned transition model outperforms a small, soundly-selected prefetch set.

We now return to the physical layout rewriter (\texttt{Clustered}) introduced in Section~\ref{sec:layout}. Our evaluation finds no scenario in which \texttt{Clustered} outperforms the default layout (\texttt{Default}) when combined with access-pattern prefetching. The best warm e2e on each workload is achieved with \texttt{Default} plus access-pattern targeting: Scattered-Zipf 452 \textmu s vs \texttt{Clustered} 523 \textmu s, Uniform-100K 509 \textmu s vs \texttt{Clustered} 701 \textmu s, and (from the tie-break-corrected batch) Tail-Mixed \texttt{Skel+10} 265 \textmu s vs \texttt{Clustered} 317 \textmu s. Each orig-vs-\texttt{Clustered} pair is same-batch. The Tail-Mixed pair uses the corrected \texttt{tiebreak\_fix} values (orig $-75\%$, \texttt{Clustered} $-63\%$). The type-aware layout produces slightly larger percentage improvements for structural prefetching (on Scattered-Zipf, \texttt{Skel-92} first-query goes from $-30\%$ to $-32\%$), but this is an artifact: the pread first-query floor under \texttt{Clustered} is 187 \textmu s versus 187 \textmu s under \texttt{Default}. The larger percentage stems entirely from a raised baseline (523 to 658 \textmu s, $+26\%$), not from a lower absolute latency. Clustering interiors at the file head pushes leaves to higher offsets, raising the cold-leaf-fault baseline. The absolute benefit of interior prefetching does not improve. A production system arrives at the same conclusion from the
opposite direction: the \texttt{.dbi} side file (Section~\ref{sec:layout}) avoids in-place reordering entirely, sidestepping precisely the displacement penalty we quantify here. The narrow conditions under which \texttt{Clustered} could be net-positive, (i) prefetching is definitely deployed, (ii) only structural prefetching is available (no access-pattern observation), and (iii) the workload is sufficiently skewed, are not simultaneously met in any of our Scattered-Zipf/Uniform-100K/Tail-Mixed cells. We therefore recommend the default layout (\texttt{Default}) for production use, and treat \texttt{Clustered} as a negative result under our evaluated workloads.

\subsection{Robustness}\label{sec:eval-robustness}

We validate the headline conclusions across six robustness axes. All results in this section use the cross-seed classifications defined in Section~\ref{sec:stats}: \textit{robust} (95\% CI excludes zero), \textit{directional} (CI crosses zero but at least 7 of 10 seeds agree in sign), and \textit{tie} (effects within the noise). Each robustness axis is evaluated on a separate batch (distinct machine-state groups). Comparisons are within-axis only. Absolute latencies may differ across axes, but the relative effects and verdicts are internally consistent.

\textbf{Workload instantiation sensitivity.} The single-workload numbers reported in Sections~\ref{sec:eval-fq} and~\ref{sec:eval-tradeoff} are point estimates from one instantiation of each workload. To quantify whether these conclusions generalize across different random samples of the same distribution, we generate 10 independent random seeds for each of Scattered-Zipf, Uniform-100K, and Tail-Mixed and run the full measurement matrix on each seed. The sweep yields three findings. First, the robust, general result is the interior skeleton: \texttt{Skel} improves warm-process e2e by roughly $-25\%$ to $-30\%$ across Scattered-Zipf, Uniform-100K, and the pure-hit control (Scattered-Zipf $-25\%$, Uniform-100K $-25\%$, Tail-Hit $-28.5\%$), all with CIs excluding zero. The larger $-36\%$ belongs to Scattered-Zipf's \texttt{Skel+10} genuine-skew bonus and to Tail-Mixed's not-found-inflated \texttt{Skel}, not to the general skeleton. Second, Tail-Mixed \texttt{Skel+10}'s cross-seed mean is $-55\%$ $[-67, -43]$ but bimodal (Section~\ref{sec:eval-attribution}). Its magnitude is not stable and reflects Tail-Mixed's not-found concentration rather than a general access-pattern win. Third, the structural heuristic \texttt{Skel-5} does not survive: on Scattered-Zipf it is \textit{tie} ($-5\%$, CI crosses zero, 6/10), and on Uniform-100K the verdict is \textit{directional}, toward regression: 8 of 10 seeds worsen (median $+5\%$), with the cross-seed mean pulled to $-1\%$ by two strongly improving seeds. The structural heuristic is not merely unstable but, in most instantiations, actively harmful. The robustness of the headline is carried entirely by access-pattern strategies. Structural prefetching without access observation is unreliable.

The methodological lesson from the 10-seed sweep is that single-workload point estimates can be misleading. The main batch happened to draw a Scattered-Zipf instance whose first query was unusually cheap (baseline $\sim$520 \textmu s versus a cross-seed typical $\sim$900 \textmu s), leading to a cross-seed corrected improvement of $-36\%$ compared to the single-instantiation estimate of $-11\%$. Conversely, the same batch overestimated Uniform-100K \texttt{Skel-5} at $-34\%$, which is near zero in the cross-seed mean ($-1\%$). Both biases are only detectable with multi-seed sampling, validating the methodology of Section~\ref{sec:stats}.

\textbf{Memory pressure.} Serverless platforms enforce per-function memory caps via cgroup \texttt{MemoryMax}. When the cap falls below the working set, page reclaim competes with prefetch delivery. We sweep caps from 16 MB to 6 MB, representing 0.92$\times$ to 0.35$\times$ the working set for Scattered-Zipf and Uniform-100K. Figure~\ref{fig:ram} shows the result. The five targeted strategies (hotsets ranging from 20 KB to 2 MB) maintain 100\% delivery across all caps. Their hotsets are orders of magnitude smaller than the memory cap, so reclaim never touches them. First-query latency remains flat. In contrast, \texttt{Dump}, whose hotset is the full working set (17.7 MB), loses delivery linearly as the cap shrinks (100\% to 20\%), and its first-query latency jumps directly from 98 \textmu s to the cold baseline of approximately 500 \textmu s as soon as delivery drops below 100\%. The cache-dump approach exhibits no graceful degradation. It is all-or-nothing. For Tail-Mixed, whose working set is only $\sim$1.8 MB, the cap cannot be meaningfully reduced below the working set without hitting the measurement floor. Its RAM robustness is a deductive consequence of its hotset being smaller than any measurable cap and is not empirically tested in this axis.

\textbf{Write churn.} Serverless data stores ingest continuously between invocations. We therefore subject the database to 50,000 mutations (10 rounds of 5,000 writes) and measure the static $t=0$ hotset across 11 checkpoints. This churn batch predates the trace-order-independent tie-break correction. We therefore use it only to assess within-batch flatness across checkpoints, and treat the absolute level as diagnostic rather than as a corrected estimate of the \texttt{Skel+10} effect magnitude. Within the same pre-fix estimator and batch, the frozen plan does not show a systematic latency increase across checkpoints when the read hotspot remains stationary: on Tail-Mixed, \texttt{Skel+10} maintains $\sim$82--89 \textmu s across checkpoints (aside from one transient spike at checkpoint 4), compared to a baseline of $\sim$580 \textmu s (these absolute microseconds are pre-fix diagnostic values, not a corrected magnitude). Uniform-100K, which has no natural hot leaves, is similarly flat. Under this stationary-read-hotspot configuration, no plan refresh was needed to preserve the within-batch trend over 50k mutations. This scope is important: write churn perturbs page layout but leaves the \emph{read} hotspot in place. A read hotspot that moves over time is a distinct axis, the non-stationary Latest-Aging versus stationary Short-Scan Aging contrast examined next, under which a frozen frequency plan does decay. The corresponding figure is available in our artifact.

\textbf{Static-plan staleness under a moving hotspot.} We ask what happens when the read hotspot itself drifts, using two aging robustness workloads that are deterministic Python reconstructions of YCSB semantics (Section~\ref{sec:workloads}): Latest-Aging (read-latest), whose hotspot moves as new keys are inserted (non-stationary), and Short-Scan Aging (short-ranges), whose hotspot is stationary. A frozen $t{=}0$ plan is replayed across 11 checkpoints (10 seeds, bootstrap 95\% CI). Under Latest-Aging, the frozen \emph{frequency} plan (\texttt{Skel+10-static}) decays as the hotspot moves away from it: its warm first-query benefit erodes from $-50\%$ at checkpoint~0 to $-33\%$ at checkpoint~10, while the \emph{structural} plan (\texttt{Skel-92-static}), which covers the navigation skeleton independent of which keys are hot, holds at $-53\%$ across all checkpoints. Under the stationary Short-Scan Aging, both plans stay flat ($-53\%\!\to\!-55\%$ frequency, $-53\%\!\to\!-51\%$ structural). A static frequency plan is therefore safe only while the read hotspot is stationary. Once it moves, structural coverage is the durable choice, matching the recommendation in Table~\ref{tab:guidance}. The corresponding figure is available in our artifact.

\textbf{Cadence and MAP\_SHARED.} The threat is a prefetched hotset cooling between invocations. With \texttt{mmap(MAP\_SHARED)} a single background warmer re-warms the hotset for all processes sharing the OS page cache, at a configurable cadence. At cadence values of 1 s and 5 s, first-query latency is maintained at 26 \textmu s and 29 \textmu s, respectively. At 30 s or never, it regresses to 281--305 \textmu s, near the no-prefetch baseline. There is no single best cadence: lower cadence holds warmth at the cost of extra I/O and CPU, and the operator selects a point on this frontier from the workload's query interval.

\textbf{Database size scaling.} We scale the reference database by $10\times$ to $\sim$1 GiB (6 million rows, 263,991 pages) and rerun the experiment on a separate batch. The first-query improvement is size-robust: across the 18 (workload $\times$ strategy) cells, all cells show the same direction of improvement as on $\sim$103 MiB, and all 1 GiB cells are robust (CI excludes zero). The warm-process e2e sensitivity is concentrated on Tail-Mixed for dump-style strategies: \texttt{Dump} goes from $-9\%$ on $\sim$103 MiB to $+139\%$ on 1 GiB, and \texttt{Skel+500} goes from $-31\%$ to $+35\%$, as the resident working set for Tail-Mixed expands from 483 to 984 pages, doubling the deliver cost. In contrast, the tie-break-unaffected interior skeleton \texttt{Skel} remains a robust win on Tail-Mixed at both sizes, and the size story is carried by the deliver-cost trend rather than by any corrected leaf-selector magnitude: the cache-dump deliver trap worsens with database size while frugal skeleton prefetch stays effective. (The pre-fix \texttt{Skel+10} figures of $-70\%$/$-68\%$ at $\sim$103~MiB/1~GiB are diagnostic only. The corrected leaf-selector magnitude was not re-validated across sizes.) The corresponding figure is available in our artifact.

\subsection{Cross-Platform First-Query Effectiveness Portability}\label{sec:eval-portability}

The results so far were measured on a single workstation harness. This
experiment does not re-evaluate the end-to-end cost ordering on OpenWhisk. It
asks the narrower deployment question of whether the first-query effectiveness of
the prefetched page sets survives the FaaS runtime and host environment. We
replicated the strategy matrix on an Apache OpenWhisk deployment
(Section~\ref{sec:setup-openwhisk}) and compared it to the workstation cell by
cell, using the frozen non-pooled evidence defined in
Section~\ref{sec:setup-openwhisk}.
First-query effectiveness is the relative first-query reduction against the
same-condition baseline, $R = (\mathit{fq}_{\text{base}} -
\mathit{fq}_{\text{strat}}) / \mathit{fq}_{\text{base}}$. Only this relative
quantity is comparable across platforms, and we never compare absolute
microseconds between the two. As noted in Section~\ref{sec:setup-openwhisk}, this
fresh-handle paired first-query (a per-invocation handle mode, not the
external-warmer deployment boundary) is a deployment-feasibility and first-query
effectiveness signal, not an absolute strategy-performance estimate. This
validation runs on a separate commodity x86 host with its own consumer NVMe and a
different kernel. It exercises the FaaS runtime together with its host and does
not isolate the container-runtime dimension or establish hardware portability.
Over the strategy$\times$workload cells both platforms ran, coverage is complete:
all 65 of 65 cells, comprising 55 first-query effectiveness cells plus 10
delivery-order \texttt{LP} cells.

We classify each cell's effectiveness as effective ($R \geq 0.10$), harmful
($R \leq -0.10$), or neutral, and call a workstation cell strongly effective
when $R_{\text{ws}} \geq 0.30$. We report the per-cell rank correlation over
three nested subsets: all 55 first-query cells, a high-confidence subset of 46
(dropping low-sample cells), and a high-confidence \emph{clean} subset of 42
(also dropping the 4 position-sensitive cells whose baseline-first and
target-first subsets disagree in sign). Seven of the 55 first-query cells were re-measured with position-balanced
replications after their initial measurements showed low sample counts or
pair-position imbalance. The campaigns and the per-cell precedence rule were
specified before the replication runs. Every OpenWhisk statistic reported in this
paper uses the revised measurements for those seven cells and the original
measurements for the remaining 48. The historical values are retained in the
artifact for provenance.

Within the 55-cell first-query domain, all 41 cells that are strongly effective
on the workstation remain effective under OpenWhisk (41 of 41,
Figure~\ref{fig:portability}). The two platforms' per-cell first-query
effectiveness ranks show substantial but imperfect agreement, correlating at
Spearman $\rho \approx 0.76$ over all 55 cells, $0.79$ over the high-confidence
46, and $0.81$ over the clean 42. Per-cell first-query effectiveness direction
agrees in 46 of 55 cells, and the median absolute effectiveness gap is $|\Delta
R| \approx 0.11$. Table~\ref{tab:portability} breaks the correlation down by
workload. First-query effectiveness shows substantial overall cross-platform rank
agreement, with workload-level agreement varying by regime. The one weak
correlation (Tail-Hit, $\rho \approx 0.25$) is the pure-hit control whose cells
are all effective on both platforms with little rank spread to correlate, so its
low $\rho$ is not a portability failure.

Three principles summarize the takeaways. First, the cache-dump strategy (\texttt{Dump}) minimizes first-query latency but is unsuitable for the cold-start critical path in all but the smallest working-set scenarios. Its deliver overhead dominates response time, and its value lies in steady-state average latency rather than in user-facing first-query response. Moreover, even the small-working-set exception on Tail-Mixed erodes as the database grows (Section~\ref{sec:eval-robustness}), further reinforcing that cache-dump is not a robust critical-path solution. Second, the interior skeleton on the original layout (\texttt{Default} + \texttt{Skel}) is the recommended default for warm-process serverless deployments: it delivers consistent end-to-end improvements across all three workload types, survives cross-seed validation as robust, and has a memory footprint under 2 MB. A top-$K$ leaf budget (\texttt{Skel+10}) should be added \emph{only} when a stable access skew has been independently validated, and when the read hotspot moves over time, the durable choice is structural coverage or a periodic plan refresh rather than a frozen frequency plan. Third, the structural heuristic (\texttt{Skel-5}) should not be used alone. Its single-workload wins on Scattered-Zipf and Uniform-100K do not survive cross-seed validation. In most workload instantiations it is neutral or harmful. If structural prefetching is the only available mechanism, it should be paired with access-pattern observation or replaced by prefetching the full interior set ($N=92$, 368 KB), which does not depend on offset-order selection.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}\label{sec:discussion}

All experiments run on a single commodity x86 machine with a consumer-grade Gen4 NVMe SSD and a single Linux kernel version. Absolute microsecond latencies depend on the hardware, kernel, and runtime. The cost-accounting methodology is intended to transfer to platforms sharing the same warm-process, cold-data model. Whether the first-query effectiveness of the prefetched page sets carries over to a genuine FaaS runtime is an empirical question we test directly with an Apache OpenWhisk deployment (Section~\ref{sec:eval-portability}). On OpenWhisk, all 41 workstation cells with strong first-query effectiveness (relative first-query reduction $R_{\text{ws}}\ge0.30$) remain effective. Across the 55 matched first-query cells, continuous first-query effectiveness ranks show substantial but imperfect agreement (Spearman $\rho\approx0.76$ to $0.81$), with 46 of 55 categorical direction agreement. This validates deployability and first-query effectiveness portability, not cross-platform end-to-end ranking, using the frozen non-pooled evidence defined in Section~\ref{sec:setup-openwhisk}. The OpenWhisk fresh-handle paired first-query (a per-invocation handle mode, not the external-warmer deployment boundary of the cost model) is a deployment-feasibility and first-query effectiveness signal, not an absolute strategy-performance estimate. In particular, our measurements characterize the local-storage tier that production object-storage architectures interpose as a cache layer (e.g., Cloudflare D1's local-disk caching over R2~\cite{CloudflareD1}). The purely remote tier replaces the OS page cache with VFS-level range requests, changing the mechanism while preserving the dependent-fault-chain structure and multiplying per-fault cost by three to four orders of magnitude. This FaaS-runtime validation runs on a separate commodity x86 host with its own consumer NVMe and a different Linux kernel, so it exercises the container-based runtime together with its host rather than isolating the runtime dimension or establishing hardware portability. The few per-cell disagreements are confined to strategies neutral on at least one platform and to position-sensitive cells (Section~\ref{sec:eval-portability}). None is a regression of a workstation-effective strategy. Systematic portability across diverse hardware classes and to commercial FaaS providers remains future work.

Our stable \texttt{Dump} fast-path anchor exhibits approximately 10\% to 15\% session-to-session absolute drift. Individual latency cells can shift more substantially across machine-state groups. Diagnostics associate this drift with SSD internal state and CPU thermal conditions. The \texttt{Dump} anchor's first-query latency is independent of the workload key distribution, so we use it to monitor drift and anchor every effect estimate to a same-batch baseline. We never compare absolute latency across independent batches.

The workload coverage spans three distribution families, skew (Scattered-Zipf), uniformity (Uniform-100K), and mixed tail-boundary lookups (Tail-Mixed), with 10-seed sampling to cover within-family variation. It does not cover real-world mixed read-write traces or distribution families beyond these three. The RAM robustness of Tail-Mixed remains deductive rather than empirically measured, because its working set (approximately 1.8 MB) falls below the measurable cgroup pressure floor. Constructing a larger working-set variant of Tail-Mixed is future work.

The measurement methodology stops at the OS page cache layer and cannot control SSD-internal FTL behavior, garbage collection, or wear leveling. The long-term evolution of page layout under sustained churn, beyond the 50k-mutation window, remains unobserved. A FEMU-based study is a natural extension.

The warm-process model differs from a full cold reboot by approximately 1 to 3 \textmu s of CPU cache warm-up bias, less than 1\% of the cold baseline. The strict cold-start mode, which quantifies this bias precisely, is left as future work.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

Serverless functions backed by SQLite experience a persistent cold-start bottleneck when the OS page cache is empty after idle periods, making the first query orders of magnitude slower than subsequent ones. Our central argument is methodological: evaluating cold-start prefetch by post-prefetch first-query latency alone is insufficient, because the prefetch preprocessing itself lies on the user-visible critical path. Once that preprocessing is charged (decomposed into open and deliver terms under a warm-process/integrated and an external-warmer deployment boundary), the strategy ranking changes: the cache-dump strategy that minimizes first-query latency, \texttt{Dump}, regresses end-to-end cold starts by roughly an order of magnitude on broad workloads due to deliver overhead of 0.8 to 7 ms. The design point that survives this accounting is frugal coverage. A small-footprint set covering the interior navigation path (\texttt{Skel}, footprint under 2 MB) achieves roughly $25$ to $30\%$ warm-process end-to-end improvement across the evaluated pure-hit workloads, robust across seeds. Hot leaves help only under an independently verified concentrated hotspot (raising Scattered-Zipf from about $-25\%$ to $-36\%$ under genuine Zipfian skew). Crucially, at matched footprint a frequency-ranked dump is statistically indistinguishable from the type-aware selector, so what carries the result is footprint and sound coverage rather than selector sophistication. Structural page-type knowledge remains valuable as an interpretable coverage guarantee and because structural coverage stays stable when the read hotspot moves, whereas a frozen frequency plan decays. A mixed tail-boundary workload whose range is roughly half not-found high-key probes yields a larger but narrowly scoped, bimodal result driven by a rightmost-leaf hotspot. A matched pure-hit control returns to the coverage regime. We additionally show that physical page reordering does not translate into end-to-end wins, and that the small offset-ordered structural heuristic \texttt{Skel-5}, distinct from observed interior coverage (\texttt{Skel}), is unreliable, falling into tie or directional-toward-regression under cross-seed validation. The unreliability is specific to that small offset-order heuristic, not to interior coverage. Consequently, no single strategy stack is universally optimal: the right choice is conditional on the deployment model and the workload's hotspot structure, with interior-navigation coverage on the original layout as the robust default. Finally, the end-to-end design conclusions are established by the controlled workstation experiments. The separate-host OpenWhisk deployment provides complementary evidence that the mechanism remains deployable and that its first-query effectiveness signal is broadly consistent on a genuine FaaS runtime. The deployment covers all 65 workstation-matched cells, including 55 first-query effectiveness cells and 10 \texttt{LP} delivery-order cells. Within the 55-cell first-query domain, all 41 strong-workstation cells remain effective, and first-query effectiveness ranks correlate at Spearman $\rho\approx0.76$ to $0.81$. This is a first-query effectiveness portability result, not a cross-platform end-to-end or hardware-portability claim.

To our knowledge, this is the first work to quantify the first-query versus end-to-end trade-off in SQLite-backed serverless cold starts at OS-syscall granularity. The framework is non-intrusive, operating entirely at the application layer with standard OS interfaces, and is designed to operate within the execution constraints of FaaS and edge environments.

For future work, the page-type knowledge that drives our selection lever can be pushed below the file abstraction: NVMe Stream Directives or ZNS can physically segregate interior and leaf pages at the SSD level, achieving type-aware placement without the leaf-displacement penalty that made file-level reordering a negative result, and preventing garbage collection from perturbing layout over time. In the object-storage regime, the same skeleton-first selection maps to a VFS-level question: which pages to coalesce into a single range request, and whether to issue background read-ahead. At millisecond per-fault costs, the trade-offs we quantify become correspondingly larger. A continuous prefetch direction, maintaining hot-set residency across invocations, would extend our cold-start framework to steady-state scenarios, but this would move the design point from low-frequency, no-root application-layer operation to the high-frequency, kernel-level regime occupied by Leis et al. \cite{Leis2023}, and would require re-evaluating the deployment model accordingly. More broadly, we argue that cost accounting, not first-query latency alone, should be the evaluation standard for cold-start prefetching.
